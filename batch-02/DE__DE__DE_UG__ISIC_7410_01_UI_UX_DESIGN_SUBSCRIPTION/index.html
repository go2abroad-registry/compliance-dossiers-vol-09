The UG requires a minimum nominal share capital of EUR 1 (GmbHG § 5a(2)); the entire subscribed cash capital must be paid in full before the Commercial Register application is submitted and must be shown to be at the free disposal of the managing director by means of a bank confirmation. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation: the UG is a German corporation (Kapitalgesellschaft) subject to corporate income tax (Körperschaftsteuer) at 15% plus solidarity surcharge (5.5% of CIT, effective 0.825%) and municipal trade tax (Gewerbesteuer; basic rate 3.5% x municipal Hebesatz, effective rate typically 7%–17%); resident corporations are taxable on worldwide income with DTT relief and automatic 95% participation exemption for qualifying dividends/capital gains under Section 8b KStG; no pass-through, no check-the-box.

Available election forms

German law provides no US-style check-the-box; available mechanisms: (1) Organschaft (corporate income tax and trade tax grouping) under Section 14 KStG requiring a profit-and-loss transfer agreement and majority voting rights; (2) VAT fiscal unity (Section 2(2) No. 2 UStG) requiring 75% financial integration; (3) Kleinunternehmer small-business VAT exemption under Section 19 UStG with waiver option under Section 19(2) UStG; (4) election of monthly or quarterly VAT advance returns; (5) mandatory automatic application of Section 8b KStG exemption; (6) advance pricing agreement (APA) with German tax authorities for cross-border transfer pricing; (7) use of the EU OSS portal for qualifying B2C digital services.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Germany is a fully convertible currency jurisdiction. The euro is a free-float currency with no restrictions on current account convertibility, capital account convertibility, or profit remittance under EU primary law (Articles 63–65 TFEU). Cross-border payment transfers are not subject to exchange control approval. The only obligation is statistical: receipts from or payments to non-EU counterparts exceeding EUR 12,500 must be reported to the Deutsche Bundesbank within seven days under AWV § 67. Profit remittance of dividends is unrestricted but attracts statutory withholding tax of 25% plus 5.5% solidarity surcharge (effectively 26.375%) unless reduced by an applicable Double Taxation Treaty; service revenue remitted by the UG is unrestricted.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Not applicable – Germany imposes no mandatory surrender of export proceeds, no forced conversion ratio, and no forex account restrictions. The UG may retain export proceeds in any foreign currency in a bank account. However, under AWV §§ 62–67, residents must report to the Deutsche Bundesbank any foreign receivable/payable position and any cross-border payment of EUR 12,500 or above for balance-of-payments statistics within seven days. There is no requirement to convert foreign exchange into euro.

Central bank supervision: Deutsche Bundesbank (Frankfurt am Main) within the Eurosystem; the European Central Bank holds primary monetary authority, while the Bundesbank administers foreign payments statistics and controls under the German Foreign Trade and Payments Ordinance (Außenwirtschaftsverordnung – AWV).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Medium-High for operational use (e.g., Wise Business, Pismo, Revolut Business) due to EU/EEA passporting of payment institution licences under PSD2. Legal constraints: an Electronic Money Institution (EMI) is not a full credit institution and cannot provide a statutory bank confirmation of share capital payment required by the German notary for UG/GmbH registration under GmbHG § 8(1) No. 3; the EMI's payment account does not benefit from the statutory German Deposit Guarantee Act (Einlagensicherungsgesetz). The UG may use an EMI for low-value operational disbursements and subscription payouts, but the initial capital contribution and tax-relevant disbursements must flow through a BaFin-licensed or European Economic Area (EEA) credit institution with a German branch relationship.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

The shareholder's initial capital contribution (Stammkapital) must be fully paid in cash before registration of the UG. The contribution is wired via SEPA (EU) or SWIFT MT103 (non-EU) to the UG's euro-denominated blocked business account at a German full bank; the payment reference must state 'Stammkapital-Einlage' together with the shareholder's name and the UG's registration number. The bank issues a confirmation of receipt (Bankbestätigung) that is submitted to the notary for the commercial register filing. Any non-EU inbound remittance of EUR 12,500 or above triggers AWV § 67 statistical reporting to the Deutsche Bundesbank within seven days, specifying the purpose code for capital participation. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal corporate income tax (Körperschaftsteuer), plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on the corporate income tax, producing a combined federal rate of 15.825%. A UG is also generally subject to municipal trade tax (Gewerbesteuer); the rate depends on the municipality and is calculated using the 3.5% federal base rate multiplied by the municipal assessment factor. The combined corporate and trade tax burden commonly approximates 30%, but no single national combined rate applies.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% withholding tax plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on the withholding tax, giving an effective domestic rate of 26.375%, before any church tax implications. A reduced treaty rate or exemption may be available, subject to the applicable treaty, beneficial-ownership and anti-abuse rules, and the required German refund or exemption procedure. Qualifying EU parent companies may potentially obtain relief under the Parent-Subsidiary Directive and German implementing law, subject to conditions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: For cross-border related-party transactions, German transfer-pricing documentation obligations generally apply. A Local File is generally required where annual cross-border transaction volume with related parties exceeds EUR 6 million for supplies of goods or EUR 600,000 for other transactions, including services, royalties, and financing. A Master File is generally required where the German entity belongs to a multinational group with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 100 million. German rules also require a transaction matrix and impose specific submission deadlines, generally 30 days after a request or commencement of a tax audit under the rules applicable from 2025. Smaller transactions may still require information sufficient to substantiate arm's-length pricing.

Economic substance scope: Germany does not have a general standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance remains material under German domestic law and treaty and EU anti-abuse rules, including beneficial-ownership analysis, the principal-purpose test where applicable, the German treaty-shopping rule in section 50d(3) of the Income Tax Act, controlled foreign company rules, and the general anti-abuse rule. Relevant evidence includes personnel, decision-making, premises, functions, assets, risks, and actual control of the income-producing activity.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate tax evasion and false tax filings

Statutory sanction: Tax evasion under section 370 of the Fiscal Code may result in imprisonment of up to five years or a fine; particularly serious cases may result in imprisonment from six months to ten years. Unpaid tax, interest, and administrative consequences remain payable. Negligent tax reduction may be an administrative offence subject to a fine under section 378.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may face personal criminal liability for their own conduct and may be liable for tax payments under section 69 of the Fiscal Code where culpable breaches of tax duties cause non-payment. Corporate liability does not automatically shield responsible individuals. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax penalty; criminal or enforcement measures depend on the specific case.
Transfer-pricing non-compliance

Statutory sanction: The tax authority may adjust income, impose additional tax and interest, and apply statutory documentation-related surcharges under section 162 of the Fiscal Code. The amount depends on the violation, the adjustment, and statutory minimums and maximums.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may be personally exposed for intentional or negligent breaches of tax duties, including under section 69 of the Fiscal Code. Criminal liability requires the elements of a criminal offence; a transfer-pricing adjustment alone does not establish criminal conduct.
Improper dividend withholding and treaty abuse

Statutory sanction: The payer may be liable for unwithheld tax, interest, and applicable penalties. Relief may be denied or recovered under domestic anti-abuse rules and the applicable treaty or EU rules. False claims may also trigger tax-evasion or other criminal investigation.

Director personal liability: A managing director responsible for withholding and remittance may face personal liability under section 69 of the Fiscal Code where the statutory conditions are met, as well as potential criminal liability for intentional misconduct.
Late filing, non-payment, and recordkeeping failures

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include late-filing surcharges, late-payment surcharges, interest, estimated assessments, coercive fines, audit adjustments, and enforcement against company assets. Applicable amounts depend on the tax, delay, and statutory rules.

Director personal liability: Managing directors must ensure proper tax compliance and recordkeeping. Personal liability may arise for culpable breach of tax payment duties under section 69 of the Fiscal Code; other civil or criminal consequences require their respective legal conditions.
Money laundering and beneficial-ownership non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Money laundering may be punishable under section 261 of the Criminal Code by imprisonment or a fine, with enhanced penalties in specified cases. Separate transparency-register and regulatory offences may attract administrative fines. The applicable sanction depends on the conduct and responsible person.

Director personal liability: Directors and other individuals may be personally prosecuted for their own participation or culpable failures where the relevant statute provides. Corporate form does not prevent individual criminal liability. Travel bans and imprisonment are not automatic and depend on a court or competent authority's decision.
