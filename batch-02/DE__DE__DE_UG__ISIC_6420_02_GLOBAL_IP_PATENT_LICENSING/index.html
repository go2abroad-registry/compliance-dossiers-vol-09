Statutory Formation & Board Resolutions

Competent registry, governing statute, capital lockup rules, and first board resolution template.

Competent corporate registry

The competent local district court (Amtsgericht) acting as the Commercial Register Court (Registergericht) for the company's registered office.

Governing statute

German Limited Liability Companies Act (Gesetz betreffend die Gesellschaften mit beschränkter Haftung, GmbHG), including sections 2 (formation and notarization), 5a (Unternehmergesellschaft), 7–8 (registration and application), 10 (register particulars), 35 (management and representation), and 39 (changes in managing directors). Commercial-register requirements also arise under the German Commercial Code (Handelsgesetzbuch, HGB), including section 12 on electronic filings.

Formation / constitutional filings

Notarized electronic application for entry in the Commercial Register (notarielle Handelsregisteranmeldung), supported by the notarized formation deed and articles of association or, where eligible, the statutory model protocol (Musterprotokoll).

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

A UG may be formed with share capital below the GmbH minimum of EUR 25,000; the nominal share capital must be at least EUR 1. The full nominal amount must be paid in cash before registration. Contributions in kind are prohibited at formation. No statutory escrow account is required; funds must be made available to the managing director for the company, and the director must confirm payment in the registration filing. The stated USD filing-fee equivalent is indicative; court fees are assessed in euros and may vary with the applicable fee schedule and exchange rate.

Funding & unlock path: No facility-specific fire, structural-load, or industrial workplace clearance is ordinarily triggered by the stated activity. If the company occupies premises or later operates a facility, applicable building, fire-safety, workplace-safety, and occupational-health requirements must be assessed for the premises and actual operations.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Federal Central Tax Office (Bundeszentralamt für Steuern – BZSt) for VAT IDs and cross-border exchanges; local tax office (Finanzamt) for income/corporate tax, trade tax, and VAT registration; official portal: ELSTER (www.elster.de)

E-government filing portal

A foreign entity must file the 'Fragebogen zur steuerlichen Erfassung' (tax registration questionnaire) via ELSTER or paper with the competent local Finanzamt to obtain a German Steuernummer. For VAT purposes, the entity separately applies to BZSt for a VAT identification number (USt-IdNr.) under §27a UStG, providing evidence of principal place of business, shareholder details, and expected German activities. If the UG has no permanent establishment but is subject to German corporate tax on certain German-source income, it must also appoint a fiscal representative or domestic agent for process service where required. The UG, once registered in the commercial register, receives automatic corporate tax identification; the registration is subject to mandatory submission of articles of association and background documents.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — A foreign entity must file the 'Fragebogen zur steuerlichen Erfassung' (tax registration questionnaire) via ELSTER or paper with the competent local Finanzamt to obtain a German Steuernummer. For VAT purposes, the entity separately applies to BZSt for a VAT identification number (USt-IdNr.) under §27a UStG, providing evidence of principal place of business, shareholder details, and expected German activities. If the UG has no permanent establishment but is subject to German corporate tax on certain German-source income, it must also appoint a fiscal representative or domestic agent for process service where required. The UG, once registered in the commercial register, receives automatic corporate tax identification; the registration is subject to mandatory submission of articles of association and background documents.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election exists in Germany. The UG is a domestic corporate entity taxable at entity level with no pass-through eligibility. Available statutory elections include: (i) tax consolidation (Organschaft) for corporation tax and VAT, requiring profit-and-loss pooling, financial integration, and a controlling participation; (ii) §4k EStG optional application concerning hybrid arrangements; (iii) ATAD-adopted hybrid mismatch mandatory adjustments under §4j EStG; (iv) allocation and apportionment elections for permanent establishment trade tax; (v) election to reclaim input VAT via the German VAT refund procedure under §18 Abs. 9 UStG for non-established entrepreneurs. No IP box, patent box, or similar preferential intangible royalty regime is available; foreign IP holding income is taxed as ordinary corporate income.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

19%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

Foreign suppliers without domestic establishment generally have no small-entity exemption and become VAT-registered when supplying taxable supplies in Germany, subject to reverse-charge for cross-border B2B services. For domestic small entrepreneurs, §19 UStG exempts turnover below EUR 25,000 prior-year and EUR 100,000 current-year forecast; for distance sales of goods to German consumers, the registration threshold is EUR 100,000 per calendar year. The UG engaged in IP licensing to German corporate customers will typically rely on reverse-charge by the German recipient and need not obtain a local VAT ID for those royalties, but must register if supplies are made to private consumers or via local establishment.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Export of movable goods from Germany to a third-country destination qualifies for a 0% VAT rate under §6 UStG provided that the goods physically leave the EU and the exporter retains documentary proof of transport to the export destination. For cross-border B2B services, including royalties from patent and trademark licensing, the supply is generally outside the scope of German VAT if the recipient is a business established outside the EU (place of supply under §3a UStG); no German VAT is charged and the German licensor may still claim input VAT on related costs. For B2B supplies to EU customers, the recipient must self-assess VAT in its own jurisdiction under the reverse-charge directive, and zero-rating/outside-scope treatment requires the licensor to hold the EU recipient's valid VAT ID. For B2C supplies of digital or licensing services, German VAT may be due if the customer is habitually resident in Germany; no zero-rating applies.

  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill / CMR consignment note evidencing physical departure from Germany

Germany mandates structured e-invoices (E-Rechnung) for all B2B domestic supplies from 1 January 2025; the UG must be able to receive and, going forward, issue XML-format e-invoices (XRechnung or ZUGFeRD/Factur-X). There is no central real-time fiscal clearance, no API tax clearance platform, and no teletax digital stamping. Transmission must occur via the Peppol network, another electronic data interchange (EDI), or secure email providing machine-readable content; paper or PDF-only invoices are not compliant for German B2B transactions. Non-resident suppliers may issue foreign-format invoices but must include the German recipient's VAT ID and indicate reverse-charge under §13b UStG. Tax authorities expect automated readability during audits; absence of compliant e-invoicing may result in VAT reassessment and penalty.

Export zero-rating and outside-scope treatment are strictly audited. If documentation is incomplete or the stated export/outside-scope conditions are not supported, the tax office retrospectively assesses German VAT at 19% plus statutory late-payment interest (§233a AO). Deliberate fraudulent claims constitute criminal tax evasion under §370 AO, punishable by fines and imprisonment of up to five years (or up to ten years in aggravated cases), with additional confiscation of economic benefit. The tax administration may extend the standard four-year assessment period to seven years for negligent understatement or ten years for fraud, and may also impose penalty surcharges and deny the licensor's input VAT deduction. In group cross-border licensing settings, the German tax court may recharacterize royalty arrangements under ATAD and §42 AO if zero-rating was obtained without genuine economic substance, leading to clawback of the VAT refund, corporate income tax and trade tax surcharges.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The euro is fully convertible for current-account and capital-account transactions. Germany generally permits unrestricted receipt, holding, conversion, and remittance of foreign currency and euro-denominated funds. A German UG may remit dividends, royalties, interest, loan repayments, and liquidation proceeds to non-residents, subject to distributable-profit rules, solvency and capital-maintenance requirements, arm's-length transfer-pricing requirements, withholding-tax rules, treaty relief procedures, beneficial-ownership verification, anti-money-laundering controls, and applicable tax reporting. Dividend distributions require properly approved annual accounts or an otherwise legally permissible interim distribution and cannot impair subscribed capital or statutory reserves. Royalties and interest must be supported by genuine agreements, beneficial ownership, transfer-pricing documentation, and evidence of performance. German withholding tax may apply to dividends and certain royalty or interest payments, with possible relief under an applicable double-tax treaty or EU directive subject to statutory substance, anti-abuse, and procedural requirements. Cross-border payments may also trigger German foreign-trade reporting, statistical reporting, sanctions screening, and bank-specific source-of-funds reviews.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Germany has no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement and no mandatory conversion ratio. Exporters may retain foreign-currency proceeds offshore or in German accounts, subject to contractual, tax, accounting, sanctions, and anti-money-laundering obligations. German residents must comply with applicable foreign-trade payment reporting under the Foreign Trade and Payments Regulation (Außenwirtschaftsverordnung, AWV), including statistical reports for qualifying cross-border payments and positions. The general reporting threshold for certain payment reports was increased to EUR 50,000 from 1 January 2025, subject to statutory exclusions and reporting-form requirements; the company must confirm the applicable threshold, transaction category, and filing channel with the Bundesbank at the time of payment. AWV reporting is a reporting obligation, not a currency-conversion or repatriation requirement.

Central bank supervision: Deutsche Bundesbank, operating within the Eurosystem under the European Central Bank framework. Banking supervision is principally conducted by the Federal Financial Supervisory Authority (BaFin), the European Central Bank for significant institutions, and the Bundesbank for designated supervisory functions. Germany has no separate foreign exchange administration imposing general exchange controls.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium to High for payment collection, SEPA transfers, multi-currency wallets, and routine royalty settlements, but not a complete substitute for a German bank. An electronic-money institution or payment institution licensed in Germany or another EEA state may provide services under applicable PSD2 passporting arrangements, subject to its licence scope, safeguarding model, transaction limits, correspondent access, sanctions controls, and acceptance of intellectual-property holding companies. An offshore digital EMI without an EEA licence generally cannot lawfully provide regulated payment services to German customers on a cross-border basis without an applicable legal basis and regulatory authorisation. An EMI account normally does not replace a German statutory capital-contribution account required for UG formation, may not provide a formal bank confirmation accepted by the notary or commercial register, and may restrict shareholder capital injections, dividend payments, high-value royalty flows, and third-party collections.

German and EU requirements may include MiFID II conduct classification, EMIR reporting, clearing, margin, risk-mitigation, trade-confirmation, and recordkeeping obligations. The UG should maintain a board-approved hedging policy, delegated authority matrix, exposure register, hedge-accounting assessment under the applicable accounting framework, independent valuation, collateral monitoring, and periodic effectiveness review.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium to High for payment collection, SEPA transfers, multi-currency wallets, and routine royalty settlements, but not a complete substitute for a German bank. An electronic-money institution or payment institution licensed in Germany or another EEA state may provide services under applicable PSD2 passporting arrangements, subject to its licence scope, safeguarding model, transaction limits, correspondent access, sanctions controls, and acceptance of intellectual-property holding companies. An offshore digital EMI without an EEA licence generally cannot lawfully provide regulated payment services to German customers on a cross-border basis without an applicable legal basis and regulatory authorisation. An EMI account normally does not replace a German statutory capital-contribution account required for UG formation, may not provide a formal bank confirmation accepted by the notary or commercial register, and may restrict shareholder capital injections, dividend payments, high-value royalty flows, and third-party collections.
