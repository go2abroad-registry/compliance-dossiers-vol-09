UBO Ultimate Beneficial Owner Screening Rules Official Registry Only

>=25% ownership / control

Applicable AML statute: Primary AML statute: Geldwäschegesetz (GwG), especially §§ 3, 10, 11, 19–23 and 43. Beneficial ownership is generally based on direct or indirect ownership of more than 25% of capital or voting rights, or the exercise of comparable control; if no natural person is identified after appropriate investigation, the statutory fallback is the managing director. A German UG must obtain, retain, and report beneficial-owner information to the Transparenzregister under GwG §§ 20–20a. Corporate formation, representation, and commercial-register filings are governed principally by the GmbH-Gesetz (GmbHG), including §§ 2 and 5a, and the Handelsgesetzbuch (HGB).
Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

A German UG must have a German registered office and a domestic business address capable of service and entry in the commercial register. A virtual-office arrangement is not categorically prohibited, but it must provide a valid domestic service address and must not be a fictitious address. For commercial CGI and animation, a genuine address with reliable document handling may be sufficient at formation; banks, tax authorities, counterparties, or licensing bodies may require evidence of actual operations. The registered office does not by itself establish tax residence or adequate substance.

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

An UG may be formed with share capital of at least EUR 1. The subscribed cash capital must be paid in full before the registration application is filed. Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Federal Central Tax Office (Bundeszentralamt für Steuern, BZSt) for VAT identification numbers and cross-border VAT matters; local tax office (Finanzamt) at the UG's seat for corporate income tax, trade tax, payroll tax and VAT; official filing portal ELSTER (www.elster.de)

E-government filing portal

After notarial incorporation and registration in the Commercial Register (Handelsregister), the UG's managing director files the statutory tax registration questionnaire via ELSTER; the local Finanzamt issues the tax number (Steuernummer) and registers the entity for VAT; the BZSt issues the EU VAT identification number (Umsatzsteuer-Identifikationsnummer) on application for intra-EU supplies. Non-resident entities with German VAT nexus use the simplified BZSt registration procedure without a domestic seat.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — After notarial incorporation and registration in the Commercial Register (Handelsregister), the UG's managing director files the statutory tax registration questionnaire via ELSTER; the local Finanzamt issues the tax number (Steuernummer) and registers the entity for VAT; the BZSt issues the EU VAT identification number (Umsatzsteuer-Identifikationsnummer) on application for intra-EU supplies. Non-resident entities with German VAT nexus use the simplified BZSt registration procedure without a domestic seat.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box classification exists; the UG is a body corporate taxed at entity level under the Corporate Income Tax Act (KStG) and Trade Tax Act (GewStG). Available elections include VAT consolidation (Organschaft) under Section 2(2) No. 2 UStG, the small-enterprise VAT exemption under Section 19 UStG, and the option for degressive depreciation under Section 7(2) EStG. Hybrid mismatch remedies under Section 4k EStG are mandatory with no elective carve-out.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

19%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

No general registration threshold; any entrepreneur under Section 2 UStG must register, with no minimum turnover. The Section 19 UStG small-enterprise exemption applies only below EUR 25,000 prior-year turnover and EUR 100,000 current-year estimated turnover; non-resident suppliers have no threshold and must register immediately upon establishing German VAT nexus.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods to third countries qualify for 0% VAT under Sections 4 No. 1 and 6 UStG, requiring physical export from the EU; intra-EU dispatches to a valid VAT ID qualify under Section 6a UStG. Cross-border B2B services, including CGI animation and VFX production, follow the recipient-location rule of Section 3a(2) UStG and fall outside German VAT scope; B2C remote digital services to EU consumers fall within the OSS scheme, while services to third-country consumers are outside scope. No separate zero-rating certificate is needed for services; invoice, contract, and payment evidence must prove the recipient's business establishment.

  • Customs Export Declaration (ATLAS Ausfuhranmeldung with Export Accompanying Document)
  • Bill of Lading, Air Waybill or CMR consignment note
  • Inward Remittance Certificate via Swift Wire or Wise B2B (bank/SWIFT MT103 evidence)

Mandatory structured e-invoicing (E-Rechnung) in XRechnung or ZUGFeRD 2.0 format for B2B domestic supplies: receipt capability required from 1 January 2025, outbound issuance required from 1 January 2027, with EDI exemptions until 1 January 2028. No real-time fiscal clearance, digital stamping, or API clearance exists; VAT returns are filed electronically via ELSTER with pre-filled data, and intra-EU recapitulative statements are filed through the BZSt portal.

German tax audit practice requires complete export evidence within 180 days; missing or falsified documents trigger retrospective VAT assessment at 19% with default interest of 0.15% per month under Section 233a AO. Fraudulent zero-rating claims constitute criminal VAT evasion under Section 370 AO punishable by up to 5 years' imprisonment (6 months to 10 years in aggravated cases), administrative fines under Section 26a UStG up to EUR 5,000, and personal liability of the UG's managing director under Section 69 AO.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Germany is a euro-area member state with full currency convertibility on both the current account and the capital account. There is no approval, quota, licensing, exchange-allocation, or prior-authorization regime for trade-related payments (imports of software, rendering services, and production equipment) or for capital movements (inbound equity contributions from non-resident shareholders, outbound dividend distributions, and shareholder-loan repayments). Profit remittance is not exchange-controlled: the UG may repatriate dividends or dissolve and transfer liquidation proceeds to any EU or third-country shareholder without FX authorization. The only non-FX hurdles are fiscal (German dividend withholding tax of 26.375 percent including solidarity surcharge for resident individuals, or 25 percent plus surcharge for non-resident shareholders, subject to treaty relief) and documentary (bank CRS, FATCA, UBO, and source-of-funds forms). EU and domestic sanctions screening under Regulation (EU) No 833/2014 and the AWV applies to counterparties, but commercial 3D animation and VFX production is not a restricted or dual-use sector and faces no sectoral capital controls.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Germany imposes no mandatory surrender of export proceeds, no minimum domestic conversion ratio, and no repatriation deadline for foreign-currency receivables. The UG may retain export earnings in USD, GBP, or other currencies on German or foreign bank accounts without restriction. The only binding obligation is statistical: every cross-border payment exceeding the AWV reporting threshold (currently EUR 12,500 under Section 67 AWV, as commonly applied; the exact gazetted threshold must be confirmed with the collecting bank because the Bundesbank periodically adjusts reporting limits) must be reported to the Deutsche Bundesbank for balance-of-payments statistics. In practice, the ordering or beneficiary bank files the AWV report on behalf of the customer once the customer has supplied the standardized international-transactions purpose code (Zahlungszweck).

Central bank supervision: Deutsche Bundesbank (central bank of the Federal Republic of Germany) is the competent authority for external-sector statistics and cross-border payment reporting under the German Foreign Trade and Payments Act (AWG) and the Foreign Trade and Payments Ordinance (AWV). The Federal Ministry for Economic Affairs and Climate Action (BMWK) administers the AWG/AWV; BaFin (Bundesanstalt fuer Finanzdienstleistungsaufsicht) supervises credit institutions, payment institutions, and e-money institutions for banking, payment-services, and anti-money-laundering compliance. Free movement of capital and payments is additionally guaranteed by Article 63 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Airwallex Fit: High

Feasibility: High as a supplementary cross-border payment and conversion rail, but Low/Medium as the sole treasury and escrow banking layer for the UG. EU-licensed e-money institutions (e.g., the European entity of Wise, Airwallex, and Revolut Business) may lawfully passport into Germany under the revised Payment Services Directive (PSD2) via the EBA register and are supervised by BaFin under the German Payment Services Supervision Act (ZAG). Offshore or non-passported EMIs must notify BaFin before soliciting German customers; using an unlicensed provider would breach ZAG and automatically trigger bank-liability issues. The UG may use Wise B2B for cost-efficient small-value cross-currency payouts to international contractors, but the primary operational account, the capital escrow account, and the borrowing/hedging relationship must sit with a full-license KWG credit institution (Deutsche Bank, Commerzbank, UniCredit, or a Sparkasse) to obtain ring-fencing, statutory depositor protection under the Einlagensicherungsgesetz, and a bank relationship that supports documentary credits, FX forwards, and AWV reporting.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant wire-transfer protocol: (1) capital contributions from EU/EEA shareholders use SEPA Credit Transfer with remittance information 'Einlage - Handelsregister HRB ...' and the UG's AEUV/AWV purpose code; (2) non-EEA contributions use SWIFT MT103 with beneficiary account name exactly matching the registered UG legal name, Field 70 containing the AWV purpose classification (direct investment equity contribution 'Direktinvestition - Einlage' or 'Gesellschafterdarlehen'), and the reference 'DE_UG production working capital'; Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% corporate income tax (Körperschaftsteuer), plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on the corporate income tax, producing an effective federal corporate charge of 15.825%. Trade tax (Gewerbesteuer) is additional and depends on the municipal multiplier; the combined corporate and trade tax burden is commonly approximately 23% to 33%, with approximately 29.8% at a 400% trade-tax multiplier.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% German capital-yield withholding tax (Kapitalertragsteuer), plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on the withholding tax, producing an aggregate domestic withholding rate of 26.375%. Qualifying corporate shareholders may obtain relief under the German participation exemption, the EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive as implemented in Germany, or an applicable tax treaty. The EU exemption generally requires at least 10% direct participation, a minimum one-year holding period or undertaking, qualifying legal forms and subject-to-tax conditions, and satisfaction of anti-abuse rules.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: German transfer-pricing documentation is generally mandatory for extraordinary business transactions and cross-border related-party dealings. For the annual Local File threshold, documentation requirements generally apply where cross-border supplies of goods exceed EUR 6,000,000 or cross-border supplies of services and other transactions exceed EUR 600,000. A Master File is generally required for German entities belonging to a multinational group with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 100,000,000, subject to the statutory conditions. Documentation must generally be available within 60 days after a tax authority request, and within 30 days for extraordinary business transactions.

Economic substance scope: Germany has no single stand-alone economic substance regulation equivalent to the UAE ESR regime. Substance is nevertheless relevant under transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-abuse rule in section 42 AO, treaty anti-abuse provisions, the principal-purpose test, controlled foreign company rules, beneficial-ownership analysis, withholding-tax relief, anti-hybrid rules, and corporate-law management requirements. A German UG performing commercial CGI and animation services should maintain real management, personnel or suitably supervised functions, production capability or documented outsourced control, premises or operational infrastructure, decision-making records, project records, IP ownership evidence, bank-account control, and risk-bearing capacity proportionate to its activities.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Intentional Tax Fraud

Statutory sanction: Tax evasion under section 370 AO may result in imprisonment of up to five years or a criminal fine. Serious cases may result in imprisonment from six months to ten years. The evaded tax, interest, late-payment charges, and criminal-proceeds recovery remain payable. Voluntary disclosure is strictly limited, formalistic, and unavailable or ineffective after certain investigative events.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may incur personal tax liability under section 69 AO for culpable failure to satisfy the company's tax obligations, including withholding taxes. Criminal conviction may include fines, imprisonment, professional disqualification, and confiscation. Travel bans are not an automatic tax sanction but may arise from criminal procedure, bail conditions, enforcement, or immigration measures.
Late Filing, Non-Payment, and Incorrect Tax Administration

Statutory sanction: Late-filing surcharges, late-payment surcharges, interest, estimated assessments, enforcement costs, account seizure, and compulsory collection may apply. Repeated or intentional conduct can escalate into an administrative or criminal tax offense.

Director personal liability: Directors must organize the company so taxes are calculated, declared, withheld, and paid when due. Personal liability may arise for culpable breach of those duties, particularly for wage tax, capital-yield withholding tax, and VAT. Insolvency-related payment restrictions must also be observed.
Withholding Tax and Treaty-Abuse Failure

Statutory sanction: The German payer may be assessed for the unpaid withholding tax, interest, and penalties. Intentional or reckless failure to withhold can trigger criminal or administrative tax liability, and treaty benefits may be denied under anti-treaty-shopping and principal-purpose rules.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for withholding tax deficits caused by intentional or negligent failure to supervise payment controls. Civil recovery may include the entire unpaid tax and ancillary charges; imprisonment is possible where the conduct constitutes intentional tax evasion.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation and Inadequate Documentation

Statutory sanction: The tax authority may make income adjustments, impose documentation-related surcharges, deny deductions, recharacterize payments as hidden profit distributions, impose withholding tax, and apply interest. Documentation surcharges may be material and can be calculated by reference to the income adjustment where statutory conditions are met.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil liability to the company and personal tax liability where they knowingly approve unsupported arrangements or fail to maintain records. Criminal exposure arises if the adjustment was intentionally concealed or supported by false documentation.
Hidden Profit Distribution and Unlawful Shareholder Extraction

Statutory sanction: The payment may be reclassified as a hidden profit distribution, denied as a deduction, subject to capital-yield withholding tax and solidarity surcharge, and treated as a repayment or insolvency claim. Distributions may also breach UG capital-maintenance rules.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may owe restitution to the UG for prohibited payments and may be liable to creditors in insolvency. Shareholders receiving unlawful distributions may be required to repay them where statutory conditions are satisfied. Criminal liability may arise for breach of fiduciary duties, insolvency offenses, or tax evasion.
Shell Company, Nominee Management, and Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Authorities may deny deductions and treaty benefits, recharacterize transactions, impose tax assessments and withholding liabilities, and refer suspected money laundering or tax crimes for investigation. The corporate veil is not automatically pierced solely because the entity has limited capital.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil claims, tax liability, criminal investigation, asset confiscation, and management disqualification. Travel restrictions are case-specific and not automatic. Personal liability is more likely where the director is a nominal officer who knowingly facilitates unlawful conduct.
Insolvency Delay and Creditor Prejudice

Statutory sanction: Late insolvency filing can result in criminal liability, repayment claims by the insolvency administrator, creditor damages, fines, imprisonment, and director disqualification. Payments made after insolvency may be clawed back under insolvency law.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may be personally liable for prohibited post-insolvency payments, unpaid taxes, social-security contributions, and creditor losses. Criminal imprisonment is possible for intentional or reckless insolvency offenses.
Money Laundering, Beneficial-Ownership, and AML Breaches

Statutory sanction: Administrative fines, transaction restrictions, reporting obligations, account closure, confiscation, and criminal prosecution may apply. Money laundering under section 261 StGB can result in imprisonment or a criminal fine, with enhanced consequences for commercial or organized conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors and responsible officers may face personal criminal liability, confiscation of proceeds, professional consequences, and civil liability. A travel ban is not automatic but may be imposed in criminal proceedings or as an enforcement measure.
Copyright, Software, and IP Misappropriation

Statutory sanction: Civil injunctions, destruction or recall of infringing materials, damages, account of profits, license fees, legal costs, and criminal penalties for intentional commercial copyright infringement may apply. Tax deductions and royalty withholding positions may also be challenged.

Director personal liability: The UG is primarily liable, but directors may incur personal liability for their own infringement, negligent organizational failure, or knowing authorization. Criminal imprisonment or fines are possible for intentional infringement; ordinary commercial disputes do not automatically create travel restrictions.
Employment, Payroll Tax, and Social-Security Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Back taxes, employee and employer social-security contributions, interest, penalties, audits, and criminal prosecution may apply. Withholding or social-security evasion can result in imprisonment or fines.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unpaid employee social-security contributions and wage-tax withholding caused by culpable conduct. Criminal exposure is significant where underreporting is deliberate or systematic.
VAT Fraud and Cross-Border Invoice Abuse

Statutory sanction: Input-tax denial, VAT assessments, interest, late-payment charges, penalties, criminal prosecution, and confiscation may apply. Serious VAT fraud may result in imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for VAT deficits under section 69 AO and may face criminal liability for intentional VAT evasion. Company insolvency does not extinguish personal exposure caused by culpable conduct.
