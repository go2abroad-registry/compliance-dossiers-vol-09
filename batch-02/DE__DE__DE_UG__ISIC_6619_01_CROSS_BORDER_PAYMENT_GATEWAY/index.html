Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

UG (haftungsbeschränkt) requires a minimum fully paid-up share capital of at least EUR 1.00, fully subscribed and paid in cash before registration; the contribution must be at the free disposal of the managing director, typically evidenced via a notary escrow account (Notar-Anderkonto) or a blocked German business bank account (Sperrkonto) until the registration is effected. Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Bundeszentralamt für Steuern (BZSt) for VAT identification and cross-border tax matters; local Finanzamt (Tax Office) for corporate income tax, trade tax, wage tax, and VAT administration. Electronic portal: ELSTER (www.elster.de).

E-government filing portal

A German UG automatically receives a local tax number (Steuernummer) from its competent Finanzamt upon commercial register filing. For VAT purposes, the UG must separately submit Form USt 1 TA to the Bundeszentralamt für Steuern (BZSt) to obtain a VAT identification number (USt-IdNr.). Non-resident entities supplying digital or financial services in Germany register for VAT through the BZSt under Section 27a UStG, providing proof of business activity, a German fiscal representative if established outside the EU, and a German bank account; registration is typically completed within 4–6 weeks.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Germany does not maintain a check-the-box or elective pass-through regime. Available elections include (i) Organschaft (tax group) under Section 14 KStG and Section 2(2) No. 2 UStG to consolidate corporate income tax and VAT with a controlling domestic parent; (ii) the small-business VAT scheme under Section 19 UStG for turnover below the statutory thresholds; and (iii) the option to apply for a VAT deduction on financial services where otherwise partially exempt. Hybrid mismatch rules under Section 4k EStG apply mandatorily and are not elective.

Standard VAT / GST rate

19%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

EUR 22,000 (previous year) / EUR 50,000 (current year) small-business threshold under Section 19 UStG; however, for cross-border payment service providers (PSP), no de minimis threshold applies — any supply of services taxable in Germany obligates VAT registration, regardless of turnover.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods qualify for 0% VAT under Section 6 UStG when tangible goods physically depart Germany to a third-country destination and proper export proof is retained. Services supplied to business customers outside Germany are not taxable in Germany under the place-of-supply rule in Section 3a UStG; services supplied to private customers outside Germany are likewise not taxable in Germany. Payment gateway services provided to foreign merchants are normally exempt under Section 4 No. 8 UStG as financial services rather than zero-rated, and may carry a restriction on input VAT recovery.

From 1 January 2025, domestic B2B invoices must be issued as structured e-invoices (XRechnung or ZUGFeRD) transmitted via EDI or PEPPOL; Germany currently does not mandate real-time fiscal clearance, API-based tax authority clearance, or digital stamping; B2C invoices may remain in PDF or paper form with recipient consent.

Failure to substantiate export zero-rating or offshore supply treatment triggers retrospective VAT assessment at 19% on the gross remuneration, plus late-payment interest of 0.5% per month, and may result in denial of corresponding input VAT deductions. Intentional or fraudulent zero-rating claims are prosecuted under Section 370 AO (Steuerhinterziehung), punishable by imprisonment up to 10 years and monetary fines; negligent misconduct is penalized under Section 378 AO. German Customs (Zoll) and BZSt perform electronic cross-checks of export declarations and bank transaction records, and all VAT-relevant documents must be retained for at least 10 years.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Euro is fully convertible on both current and capital accounts. Germany imposes no exchange-control approval, surrender, or prior-authorization regime. Free movement of capital is guaranteed by Article 63 TFEU, with no distinction between resident and non-resident capital transactions. Profit remittance and dividend distribution are unrestricted; however, dividend payments to non-resident shareholders are generally subject to German withholding tax (Kapitalertragsteuer) of 25% plus 5.5% solidarity surcharge, unless reduced or exempted under an applicable tax treaty or the EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive (Directive 2011/96/EU). The UG may remit profits only after the satisfaction of annual financial statements and statutory reserve requirements (§ 5a GmbHG).

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Germany has no statutory surrender, repatriation, or mandatory conversion requirement for export proceeds. The only material obligation is the AWV statistical reporting duty: any resident receiving or paying a cross-border economic transaction exceeding EUR 12,500 must report it to the Deutsche Bundesbank for balance-of-payments statistics (Zahlungsbilanz-Statistik). This reporting obligation is retrospective (by the 5th working day of the subsequent calendar month) and is not a licensing or currency-control gate; failure to report attracts administrative fines.

Central bank supervision: Deutsche Bundesbank (Frankfurt am Main), operating within the Eurosystem/European System of Central Banks. Currency and exchange controls are administered pursuant to the German Foreign Trade and Payments Ordinance (Außenwirtschaftsverordnung – AWV) and the Foreign Trade and Payments Act (AWG). Licensing of payment services is concurrently supervised by Bundesanstalt für Finanzdienstleistungsaufsicht (BaFin).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Medium. German-licensed Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) under the Payment Services Supervision Act (ZAG) – including Solaris SE, Pliant GmbH, and comparable BaFin-regulated platforms – can lawfully host the UG's settlement IBAN, issue commercial e-money accounts, and provide API-based payment-processing infrastructure without a traditional universal-bank relationship. The UG itself must hold or have applied for a ZAG payment-institution licence in order to operate a cross-border merchant-acquiring gateway; a pure EMI may not warehouse merchant settlement funds or issue merchant credit on behalf of the UG. Any EMI arrangement must comply with the safeguard requirements of § 27 ZAG (client funds held in segregated trust accounts or protected by comparable insurance), with a monthly reconciliation and external audit trail. Practical viability diminishes above approximately EUR 1 million annual settlement volume, where the EMI's own risk-committee review, cross-border corridor restrictions, and capital requirements downgrade viability to Low.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Initial equity injections and shareholder loans from the non-resident parent must be wired to the UG's EUR account at a German clearing bank through SWIFT MT103 or TARGET2, using remittance/purpose code '301 – Capital Contribution' (or 'GF – Gesellschafterfinanzierung' where accepted), with a matching payment narrative identifying the UG and the purpose, such as 'Kapitalerhöhung DE_UG – 01'. The capital increase must be documented by a notarial deed and filed with the Commercial Register before the funds are released from escrow. When the inbound transfer exceeds EUR 12,500, the UG must file the AWV cross-border payment notification with the Deutsche Bundesbank within the statutory reporting period. No exchange-control pre-approval, central-bank settlement, or conversion restriction applies to the injected EUR capital. The receiving bank will verify the corporate resolution and UBO documentation before crediting the operational balance.Outbound merchant settlements, supplier payments, and trade disbursements are executed over the following rails: (i) SWIFT MT103 (serial payment) for individual high-value supplier or merchant payouts in EUR, USD, GBP; (ii) SWIFT MT202/MT202 COV for bank-to-bank cover payments and interbank liquidity across Fedwire and TARGET2; (iii) SEPA Instant Credit Transfer (SCT Inst) for sub-EUR 100,000 disbursements to EEA merchants with real-time settlement; (iv) Commercial Letters of Credit under UCP600 and documentary collections for structured trade payments to physical-goods vendors; (v) for corridors into Uganda and East Africa, a USD-denominated MT103 via a local correspondent bank (e.g., through Nairobi or Kampala), followed by local-currency disbursement in UGX through the destination PSP. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% corporation tax (Körperschaftsteuer) plus 5.5% solidarity surcharge on the corporation tax, producing an effective corporation-tax burden of 15.825%; municipal trade tax (Gewerbesteuer) generally applies separately, with the combined national and municipal burden commonly approximately 23% to 33%, depending on the municipal multiplier.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% capital-yield withholding tax plus 5.5% solidarity surcharge on the withholding tax, producing an aggregate domestic burden of 26.375%. A qualifying EU parent company may obtain exemption under the EU Parent-Subsidiary regime, generally requiring at least a 10% direct participation, a minimum one-year holding period or undertaking, qualifying legal forms, subject-to-tax requirements, and anti-abuse compliance. German treaties may reduce the dividend rate, commonly to 5% for qualifying substantial corporate holdings or 10% to 15% for other cases, but the applicable treaty must be checked separately.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

For a calendar-year taxpayer, the annual tax return is generally due by 31 July of the following year. If prepared and filed by a tax adviser under the statutory adviser extension, the normal deadline is generally the last day of February of the second following year, subject to statutory exceptions and tax-authority requests. For a non-calendar fiscal year, the deadline is calculated by reference to the end of that fiscal year. Advance tax payments are generally due on 10 March, 10 June, 10 September, and 10 December.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: For cross-border transactions with related parties, a German Local File is generally required when annual transaction volumes exceed EUR 6 million for deliveries of goods or EUR 600,000 for other transactions, including services, financing, royalties, and similar items. A Master File is generally required for a German entity belonging to a multinational group where the German entity's revenue exceeds EUR 100 million in the relevant financial year. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to a multinational group with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million. Documentation may be requested even below thresholds where the tax authority considers it necessary.

Economic substance scope: Germany has no separate universal Economic Substance Regulation regime equivalent to a standalone UAE-style ESR filing. Substance is nevertheless material under section 42 AO anti-abuse rules, section 50d(3) EStG treaty-shopping rules, German controlled-foreign-company rules, beneficial-ownership principles, permanent-establishment rules, transfer-pricing requirements, and general corporate residence analysis. The UG should maintain German decision-making capacity, appropriately qualified personnel, genuine payment-services operations, regulated functions, risk control, records, premises, banking controls, and commercial rationale proportionate to its payment-processing business. A shell or conduit structure may lose treaty relief and generate tax reassessments, penalties, interest, and potential personal liability.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Unlicensed payment services and regulatory perimeter breach

Statutory sanction: Unauthorized provision of regulated payment services may constitute a criminal or administrative offense under the ZAG, with orders to cease operations, supervisory measures, administrative fines, and potential imprisonment under the applicable offense provisions. Customer funds may also be subject to restitution, segregation, safeguarding, or insolvency-related claims.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may face personal administrative and criminal liability for intentional or negligent unauthorized operations, civil claims for customer losses, prohibition from management functions, and liability for losses caused by failure to implement licensing, safeguarding, governance, and AML controls. Corporate limited liability does not protect directors from their own misconduct.
Tax evasion and fraudulent tax returns

Statutory sanction: Tax evasion under section 370 AO is punishable by imprisonment of up to five years or a fine. Particularly serious cases may result in imprisonment from six months to ten years. Tax, interest, late-payment surcharges, and confiscation or recovery of economic benefits may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors and responsible officers may be prosecuted personally, required to pay tax shortfalls under section 69 AO where their intentional or grossly negligent breach caused the non-payment, and exposed to asset seizure, professional disqualification, and insolvency consequences. Travel restrictions may be imposed in enforcement or criminal proceedings where legally justified; imprisonment is a personal sanction.
VAT and payment-flow misreporting

Statutory sanction: Reassessment of VAT, repayment of refunds, interest, late-payment surcharges, tax-evasion or reckless-tax-reduction proceedings, and administrative penalties may apply. Incorrect VAT invoicing can create VAT liability even where the underlying tax treatment is disputed.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unpaid taxes caused by deliberate or grossly negligent failures, including failures to maintain transaction-level records and reconcile merchant settlement accounts. Civil claims and criminal prosecution may follow where false invoices or carousel-fraud arrangements are involved.
Transfer-pricing manipulation and hidden profit distributions

Statutory sanction: The tax authority may adjust taxable income, deny deductions, recharacterize payments as hidden profit distributions, impose withholding-tax liabilities, assess trade tax, charge interest, and impose documentation-related penalties. Failure to produce required records may result in estimation of the tax base and additional surcharges.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur personal tax liability for withheld taxes not remitted, damages for breach of fiduciary duties, and criminal exposure if the pricing was intentionally false or designed to evade tax. The corporate veil does not shield deliberate director misconduct.
AML, sanctions, and beneficial-ownership failure

Statutory sanction: Breaches of the German Money Laundering Act (GwG), Criminal Code, sanctions legislation, and applicable EU measures may result in supervisory orders, administrative fines, business restrictions, disgorgement, license consequences, and criminal prosecution. Money laundering under section 261 StGB may carry imprisonment or fines, with aggravated cases subject to higher imprisonment ranges.

Director personal liability: Managing directors and compliance officers may face criminal prosecution, personal fines, confiscation, management prohibition, civil claims, and liability for inadequate organization or reporting. Travel restrictions and asset freezes may arise from criminal, sanctions, or enforcement proceedings. Imprisonment is possible for qualifying intentional conduct.
Shell-company, treaty-shopping, and artificial substance arrangement

Statutory sanction: Treaty relief or EU withholding exemption may be denied, German withholding tax may be collected retroactively, and the arrangement may be challenged under section 42 AO, section 50d(3) EStG, beneficial-ownership principles, or permanent-establishment rules. Additional tax, interest, penalties, and tax-authority estimation may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unpaid withholding tax, knowingly false certifications, and damages caused by sham arrangements. Criminal tax exposure arises where the structure was intentionally used to conceal taxable income or beneficial ownership.
Improper profit distribution and UG capital-reserve violation

Statutory sanction: Unlawful distributions may be repayable to the company, may constitute shareholder or director liability, and may contribute to insolvency proceedings. Tax recharacterization as hidden profit distributions may trigger corporation tax, trade tax, and withholding tax, together with interest and penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may be required to reimburse unlawful payments, compensate creditors, and defend claims by insolvency administrators. If insolvency exists, failure to file promptly under section 15a InsO may result in criminal sanctions, personal liability, and management disqualification.
Accounting, record-retention, and audit-trail failure

Statutory sanction: Tax-base estimation, denial of deductions, late or incorrect filings, administrative fines, supervisory sanctions, and criminal consequences for intentional falsification may result. German commercial and tax records generally require multi-year retention, with the applicable period depending on the record category.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal tax liability, civil damages, insolvency claims, and criminal exposure for intentional destruction or falsification of records. Failure to supervise outsourced accounting does not automatically eliminate director responsibility.
Failure to withhold and remit cross-border tax

Statutory sanction: The German payer may become liable for the unwithheld tax, solidarity surcharge, interest, penalties, and potential criminal tax proceedings. Treaty relief may be denied retroactively where residence, beneficial ownership, substance, or anti-abuse conditions are not met.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable under section 69 AO for intentional or grossly negligent failure to withhold and pay tax. Personal prosecution, asset enforcement, civil claims, and professional disqualification may follow in serious cases.
Insolvency delay and creditor prejudice

Statutory sanction: Violations may trigger criminal liability under insolvency law, repayment claims, director-liability claims, avoidance actions, tax enforcement, and disqualification from management. The applicable filing period is generally three weeks for illiquidity and six weeks for over-indebtedness, subject to statutory conditions and the director's duty to file earlier where required.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for post-insolvency payments, creditor losses, unpaid taxes, employee-related amounts, and insolvency-administrator claims. Criminal sanctions may include imprisonment or fines, and courts may impose management bans or other professional restrictions.
