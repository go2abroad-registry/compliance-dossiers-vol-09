Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Bundeszentralamt für Steuern (BZSt) — the Federal Central Tax Office — administers VAT identification numbers (USt-IdNr.), EU and international tax matters, and central tax registration; the local Finanzamt (district tax office) is the competent registration and assessment authority for Körperschaftsteuer (corporate income tax), Gewerbesteuer (trade tax), and wage tax; the official electronic filing portal is ELSTER (www.elster.de), supplemented by the BZSt online portal for VAT-ID applications (www.bzst.de).

E-government filing portal

Upon notarial incorporation and entry in the Handelsregister, the managing director of the UG must file the 'Fragebogen zur steuerlichen Erfassung' (statutory tax registration questionnaire) via ELSTER within one month of commencing business activity; the local Finanzamt assigns a Steuernummer (corporate income and trade tax number). Separately, the UG applies for a USt-IdNr. with the BZSt using form USt 1 TW or the BZSt online portal; VAT-ID issuance normally takes two to six weeks after corporate registration and is mandatory once the UG issues invoices having VAT consequences. If a foreign-resident sole shareholder manages the UG, the filing is supplemented with additional documentation on shareholding, beneficial ownership, and registered office, and foreign founders normally mandate a German tax adviser; no capital threshold applies to the UG itself, as its statutory minimum capital is EUR 1, but cross-border permanent-establishment risk must be reviewed when the UG contracts from abroad.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — the UG (Unternehmergesellschaft haftungsbeschränkt) is a German Kapitalgesellschaft (quasi-GmbH without minimum capital) and is an autonomous corporate taxpayer subject to Körperschaftsteuer at 15%, solidarity surcharge of 5.5% on such corporate tax, and municipal Gewerbesteuer (trade tax) at a typical combined rate of 14–17%; profits are taxed at entity level, resulting distributions (dividends) are taxed at shareholder level, and the UG is not eligible for pass-through treatment in Germany.

Available election forms

Germany has no check-the-box or elective pass-through regime for corporations; a UG is per se taxable at entity level. Available statutory mechanisms include: (1) Organschaft (corporate-tax and VAT fiscal unity) election, whereby a financially, economically, and organizationally integrated UG subsidiary executes a profit-transfer agreement (Gewinnabführungsvertrag) with its controlling parent and is treated as a permanent establishment for corporate tax and as part of a single VAT taxpayer; (2) opt-out from the § 19 UStG small-business VAT exemption, binding for five calendar years; (3) option to tax real-estate supplies under § 9 UStG; (4) capital-increase election to convert the UG into a full GmbH once accumulated statutory reserves reach EUR 25,000; (5) hybrid-mismatch rules under § 4k EStG apply mandatorily and no elective mismatch regime is permitted; a U.S. owner may separately make a U.S. check-the-box election for U.S. federal tax purposes, but that does not alter the German entity-level classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

19%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

No general economic-turnover threshold for ordinary entrepreneurs: the UG must register for VAT under § 18 UStG as soon as it performs taxable business activities. The statutory small-business exemption (§ 19 UStG) permits a registered entrepreneur — including a corporation — to waive output VAT where the gross turnover of the preceding calendar year did not exceed EUR 22,000 (raised to EUR 25,000 as of 1 January 2025) and the current-year gross turnover is not expected to exceed EUR 50,000 (raised to EUR 100,000 as of 1 January 2025); once the UG waives or exceeds that threshold, the 19% standard rate applies to domestic B2C and ordinary B2B supplies. Note that ESG advisory services delivered to German entrepreneurs are subject to the reverse charge only in the specifically enumerated cases of § 13b UStG, for example the supply of greenhouse gas emission allowances; standard management consultancy remains VAT-liable on the supplier unless the B2B place-of-supply rule of § 3a (2) UStG moves the supply into the recipient’s country.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

German VAT zero-rating applies to (i) export supplies of goods to third-country destinations under § 4 No. 1(a) and § 6 UStG where the goods are physically transported out of the EU; (ii) intra-Community supplies to EU VAT-registered business customers under § 4 No. 1(b) and § 6a UStG, evidenced by validated VIES VAT IDs; and (iii) services whose place of supply is outside Germany under the B2B recipient-destination rule of § 3a (2) UStG, including corporate Scope 1–3 carbon auditing, ESG advisory, and offset brokerage to non-EU entrepreneurs, which are outside the scope of German VAT rather than formally zero-rated. The UG must retain satisfactory export proof (Ausfuhrnachweis) and delivery documentation to validate the exemption. For domestic dealings in EU greenhouse gas emission allowances, the supply is reverse-charged to the German entrepreneur recipient under § 13b UStG, so the UG issues a net invoice and does not charge output VAT.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Ausfuhranmeldung) lodged in ATLAS and the MRN (Movement Reference Number)
  • Export proof (Ausfuhrnachweis): electronic export notification with customs confirmation of exit, or carrier/receiver confirmation for low-value consignments
  • Commercial invoice with Incoterms, HS/CN commodity codes, and export delivery terms
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill / CMR consignment note / rail or postal receipt evidencing actual transport from German territory
  • Intra-Community supply bundle: delivery note or bill of lading plus validated VIES VAT ID of the EU customer for § 6a UStG purposes
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or SWIFT MT103 of the foreign-currency repatriated proceeds
  • Client engagement letter and Scope 1–3 audit report evidencing genuine B2B service delivery for zero-rated offshore services

Germany has no real-time fiscal-clearance API, continuous transaction control, or digital stamping regime. Under the Growth Opportunities Act, a phased domestic B2B e-invoicing obligation applies: from 1 January 2025 every German B2B supplier must be able to receive structured e-invoices in the EU EN 16931 format (XRechnung or ZUGFeRD 2.x); from 1 January 2027 suppliers with prior-year turnover above EUR 800,000 must issue e-invoices; from 1 January 2028 all domestic B2B invoices must be issued electronically. Paper and semantically unstructured PDF invoices remain lawful for B2C, foreign B2B, and transitional domestic B2B until the respective deadlines. Invoices must be stored in an audit-proof electronic archive for ten years under the GoBD principles; no fiscal stamp, pre-approval, or clearance API is required.

Export zero-rating is scrutinized ex-post by the Finanzamt and centrally by the BZSt and German Customs (Zoll) through export-evidence audits; defective or fraudulent evidence triggers retrospective VAT reassessment with late-payment interest of 0.5% per month (6% per annum) under § 233a AO, accountability for failed intra-Community supplies under § 6a UStG, liability for incorrect VAT shown on invoices under § 14c UStG, denial or withdrawal of refunds under §§ 18 and 59 et seq. of the AWV (foreign-trade regulations), regulatory fines under § 26c UStG, and criminal tax-evasion proceedings under § 370 AO with fines and imprisonment of up to ten years for aggravated cases; deliberate abuse of the USt-IdNr. can additionally result in BZSt revocation of the VAT ID, cancellation of export refunds, and disqualification of the UG’s German customs-simplification authorizations.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Germany maintains full convertibility for both current and capital account transactions by virtue of European Union law (Articles 63–66 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union) and the EU Capital Movements Directive. No exchange-control approval is required for capital injection, dividend distribution, profit remittance, liquidation proceeds, or repatriation of shareholder loans. The euro is freely convertible, and there is no foreign-exchange rationing, surrender obligation, or differentiated regime for non-resident shareholders. The practical compliance hurdles are documentary and statistical rather than authorisation-based: (i) the AWV reporting obligation for every cross-border payment or receipt exceeding EUR 12,500, which must be declared to the Deutsche Bundesbank within five business days of the value date; (ii) GwG/KWG client identification and beneficial-ownership documentation; and (iii) tax withholding analysis under the German Corporate Income Tax Act (KStG) and the applicable double-taxation treaty for any dividend or interest outflow. For this UG engaged in ESG carbon footprint auditing and offset brokerage, profit remittance to non-resident shareholders is legally unobstructed and requires only the standard annual tax declaration and withholding mechanics, never an FX authorisation.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None. Germany imposes no mandatory surrender of export proceeds, no statutory conversion ratio, and no timeframe within which offshore receipts must be repatriated. A German UG may lawfully hold and retain foreign-currency receivables abroad, use them for operational disbursements, or convert them at any licensed bank without Bundesbank or BaFin approval. The sole binding requirement is the statistical reporting obligation under Section 67 AWV: any cross-border receipt (including consultancy fees and offset-brokerage commissions) exceeding EUR 12,500 must be reported to the Deutsche Bundesbank within five working days of value date. Banks typically discharge this automatically through the international-transaction statistics module, but the UG must ensure its payment instructions carry sufficient purpose codes and reference data so that the receipt is correctly classified as a service fee rather than financing flow. There is no inbound clearing restriction, no forced sale of export currency, and no centralised pool into which proceeds must be routed.

Central bank supervision: Deutsche Bundesbank (the German central bank) and the Federal Financial Supervisory Authority (BaFin – Bundesanstalt für Finanzdienstleistungsaufsicht). The Bundesbank exercises monetary policy, operates TARGET2 participation, and administers foreign trade and payments statistics under the Foreign Trade and Payments Ordinance (Außenwirtschaftsverordnung, AWV). BaFin supervises credit institutions, EMIs, and payment institutions for prudential and anti-money-laundering compliance under the German Banking Act (KWG) and the Anti-Money Laundering Act (Geldwäschegesetz, GwG). Both institutions jointly enforce cross-border reporting thresholds, with the Bundesbank as statistical recipient and BaFin as the licensing and enforcement authority.

Airwallex Fit: High

Rating: MEDIUM-HIGH as a supplementary low-cost FX and disbursement corridor; LOW as the sole treasury and settlement rail. Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

All inbound euro wires settle final through TARGET2. The compliant routing protocol is: (i) the foreign shareholder instructs its issuing bank to transmit a SWIFT MT103 to the UG's German IBAN with the value date and Unstructured Field 70 carrying purpose code '00120 – Capital Contribution (Stammeinlage/Einlage)' plus the notarial file reference; (ii) the receiving credit institution verifies name-match and, if the capital is being constituted, credits the Capital Escrow Account (Sperrkonto) and furnishes the notary with a bank confirmation of free disposal; (iii) the UG must file, or ensure the bank files, the AWV cross-border payment statistics declaration with Deutsche Bundesbank within five working days where the contribution exceeds EUR 12,500; (iv) where the injection is structured as shareholder loan rather than equity, the transfer should carry purpose code '11000 – Shareholder Loan' or '12000 – Capital Reserve' to avoid misclassification as operating revenue; (v) for in-kind contributions or subsequent capital increases, a notarised deed and the bank's crediting confirmation must be matched prior to disbursement; and (vi) the full trail – MT103 reference, IBAN credit, bank confirmation, notarial certificate, and Transparenzregister update – must be archived as part of the ESG carbon-accounting audit workpapers. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% corporation tax under the Körperschaftsteuergesetz, plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on corporation tax, producing a 15.825% federal corporate tax burden before municipal trade tax. Trade tax is additional and depends on the municipality's Hebesatz; the typical combined corporation tax, solidarity surcharge, and trade tax burden is approximately 29.8% to 33%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% capital gains tax plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on that tax, producing an effective domestic withholding rate of 26.375%, before treaty or statutory relief. A UG must also observe statutory reserve accumulation requirements: generally 25% of annual net profit, reduced by loss carryforwards, must be placed into the legal reserve until the statutory capital threshold for conversion into a GmbH is reached.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The annual return is generally due seven months after the end of the fiscal year. For a calendar-year taxpayer, the ordinary deadline is 31 July of the following year. Where a tax adviser is appointed, the statutory extension is generally available to 14 months after fiscal year-end, subject to statutory exceptions and possible administrative restrictions. Tax prepayments are generally due on 10 March, 10 June, 10 September, and 10 December.

Withholding tax must generally be declared and paid by the 10th day after the end of the month in which the capital income was paid or deemed paid. Specific deadlines depend on the payment event and the applicable withholding regime.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and fraudulent tax return

Statutory sanction: Tax evasion under Section 370 AO is punishable by a fine or imprisonment of up to five years; particularly serious cases may carry imprisonment from six months to ten years. Tax, interest, and recovery of the evaded amount remain payable.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may face criminal prosecution, personal tax liability for culpable failure to pay or withhold tax under Section 69 AO, confiscation, professional disqualification, and civil claims. Travel restrictions are not automatic and require a specific legal basis; imprisonment is possible in serious criminal cases.
Late filing, non-payment, and withholding-tax default

Statutory sanction: Late-filing penalties, late-payment surcharges, interest, enforcement measures, and personal liability of responsible officers may apply. Intentional non-remittance may constitute tax evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable where they intentionally or grossly negligently breach tax payment or withholding duties. Civil restitution and enforcement against personal assets may follow.
Hidden profit distribution and shareholder extraction

Statutory sanction: The amount may be reclassified as a hidden profit distribution, denied as a deduction, subjected to withholding tax, solidarity surcharge, interest, and penalties, and included in the shareholder's taxable income.

Director personal liability: Directors may face corporate restitution claims, liability for unpaid withholding tax, breach-of-duty claims, and insolvency-related liability if distributions impair the UG's ability to pay creditors.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: The tax authority may make income adjustments, impose documentation surcharges generally ranging from EUR 5,000 to EUR 1 million depending on the breach, deny deductions, assess interest, and apply withholding tax where recharacterization results in a distribution.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for deliberate or grossly negligent tax losses and may face criminal tax proceedings where the pricing was knowingly false or designed to evade tax.
Shell company, conduit, and treaty abuse

Statutory sanction: Treaty or EU relief may be denied under Section 50d(3) EStG and anti-abuse principles. Tax may be reassessed at domestic rates with interest, penalties, and possible criminal consequences if information was intentionally falsified.

Director personal liability: Directors may face tax liability, civil claims, and criminal exposure where they knowingly operated a sham structure. A travel ban is not an automatic consequence of shell-company status.
Money laundering and beneficial-owner concealment

Statutory sanction: Money laundering under Section 261 StGB may result in fines or imprisonment, generally up to five years and, in aggravated cases, higher penalties. GwG breaches may trigger administrative fines, potentially up to EUR 1 million or higher amounts for regulated entities under applicable provisions, plus supervisory measures.

Director personal liability: Directors and compliance officers may face criminal liability, confiscation, civil restitution, management bans, and personal exposure for intentional or negligent compliance failures.
False carbon, ESG, or offset claims

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include civil damages, injunctions, withdrawal of misleading advertising, contractual rescission, competition-law penalties, accounting corrections, and criminal fraud liability where deception caused financial loss. Specific sanctions depend on the claim, customer, and applicable regulatory framework.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil liability for misleading statements, breach of organizational duties, insolvency claims, and criminal fraud exposure where conduct was intentional.
VAT carousel, fictitious invoices, and improper input-tax recovery

Statutory sanction: Input VAT may be denied, VAT may be assessed with interest and penalties, and intentional conduct may constitute VAT evasion under Section 370 AO. Serious organized fraud may carry severe criminal penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unpaid VAT and face criminal prosecution, confiscation, and civil recovery claims.
Insolvency and wrongful distributions

Statutory sanction: Failure to file for insolvency without undue delay may constitute a criminal offense under Section 15a InsO, potentially punishable by imprisonment of up to three years or a fine. Improper payments may be recoverable by the insolvency administrator.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may be personally liable for post-insolvency payments, creditor losses, unpaid taxes, social-security contributions, and restitution of unlawful distributions. Travel restrictions may be imposed only under specific insolvency or criminal procedures.
Accounting, record-retention, and audit-trail failure

Statutory sanction: The tax authority may estimate taxable income, deny deductions, impose documentation penalties, assess tax and interest, and refer deliberate misstatements for criminal investigation.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal liability for negligent record-keeping, tax losses, shareholder or creditor claims, and criminal exposure where records were falsified or destroyed.
Social-security and payroll non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Back payments, interest, contribution surcharges, fines, and criminal liability under applicable social-security and withholding provisions may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unpaid employee social-security contributions and payroll taxes and may face criminal prosecution for intentional withholding failures.
