Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum paid-up capital applies to LLC formation under Law No. 159 of 1981; the founders freely fix the capital, which must be fully subscribed and deposited into a temporary escrow account at a licensed Egyptian bank before GAFI registration, evidenced by an official capital deposit certificate. Standard VAT / GST rate

14%

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rated for exported goods physically leaving Egypt under a customs export declaration; zero-rated for services supplied to a non-resident and consumed outside Egypt, subject to documentary evidence. The exporter must be VAT-registered and issue a valid e-invoice reflecting zero-rate; goods must be shipped within the period prescribed by the Customs Law; services must be connected to a real overseas contract and utilized abroad.

  • Customs Export Declaration (declaration no. and date, customs release certificate)
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill evidencing actual shipment from Egypt to foreign buyer
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (IRC) confirming foreign currency proceeds credited to the Egyptian exporter’s bank account through an authorized bank
  • Commercial invoice issued under e-invoicing rules
  • Purchase orders/international sales contract and, where applicable, certificate of origin

ETA mandates e-invoicing and e-receipts through the national electronic invoice portal. Taxpayers above turnover thresholds (phased per ETA decisions) must integrate APIs for real-time submission and XML/JSON validation. A unique invoice code (UUID and QR code) is issued by the ETA after automated clearance; clearance is near-real-time with pre-issuance validation for large taxpayers, and stamping/QR codes are mandatory on printed copies.

The ETA and Customs Authority jointly audit export consignment files; failure to substantiate physical export or remittance within the 180-day repatriation window triggers reversal of zero-rating with VAT at 14%, plus late payment interest charged at the Central Bank lending rate plus 2% per month or fraction thereof; fraudulent claims carry penalties of up to double the tax due and imprisonment under VAT Law Article 44, Customs Law smuggling penalties, and administrative stop on the exporter's e-invoicing profile.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Egypt operates a de facto current-account convertibility regime for trade, freight, insurance and after-tax profit remittances; licensed banks may execute import payments and profit transfers without individual CBE pre-approval provided the underlying documents are complete. Capital-account transactions remain subject to enhanced validation under Law No. 194 of 2020 on the Central Bank and Banking System and through GAFI registration. The EGP is not freely convertible on capital account: foreign capital contributions, liquidation proceeds and cross-currency swaps require the bank to file a capital-entry or capital-repatriation reference with the CBE and GAFI. Profit remittance hurdles are primarily documentary: the Egyptian bank must hold audited financial statements, a valid tax card, proof of dividend withholding tax settlement, and evidence that the EGP equivalent was purchased at the bank's dealing-room rate with no parallel-market source. Since the 2024 exchange-rate liberalisation, the CBE no longer publishes a fixed official rate, but temporary FX scarcity may still create bank-level rationing and postponement of discretionary remittances.

Export proceeds surrender rules

The CBE's export-proceeds rules require all export receipts to be repatriated to Egypt through a licensed Egyptian bank and credited to the exporter's foreign-currency account. The standard statutory collection timeline is the invoice tenor or the maturity of the export letter of credit, with a maximum banking-period limit normally not exceeding 180 days from the date of the bill of lading; after that period the bank must escalate the position to the CBE and the exporter must transfer the proceeds immediately. The mandatory conversion ratio is currently nil because Egypt maintains a unified, market-determined exchange rate; exporters may keep 100% of the proceeds in foreign currency. However, the CBE retains statutory power under exceptional FX-control instructions to impose a temporary surrender ratio; in such a declaration, exporters may be required to convert 100% of new export receipts into EGP within the period fixed by the CBE circular.

Central bank supervision: Central Bank of Egypt (CBE) - General Administration of Foreign Exchange; banking supervision is exercised through the CBE Banking Control Sector, with capital-account coordination delegated to the General Authority for Investment and Free Zones (GAFI).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low. Egypt does not recognise offshore digital EMI platforms as receiving banks for import settlements, EGP clearing, or CBE foreign-trade reporting. Only CBE-licensed banks and registered payment-service providers under Law No. 194 of 2020 can hold settlement accounts and route SWIFT MT103 or letter-of-credit messages. An offshore digital EMI may be used only as an ancillary visibility tool or virtual-card issuer for treasury expenses; it cannot replace the licensed banking channel for the LLC's frozen-beef and seafood import disbursements.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

The initial equity injection must be routed by SWIFT MT103 or MT202 cover payment from the shareholder's corporate bank to the LLC's foreign-currency account at a CBE-licensed Egyptian bank. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

22.5% corporate income tax on taxable profits of Egyptian resident companies and Egyptian-source profits of non-residents

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Generally 10% Egyptian withholding tax on dividends distributed by an Egyptian company to a non-resident shareholder, subject to domestic exemptions, listed-company rules, participation conditions, and applicable double-tax treaty relief. A 5% domestic rate may apply in specified qualifying participation or listed-share circumstances; eligibility must be tested against the relevant statutory conditions and current ETA practice.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Egyptian transfer-pricing documentation requirements generally apply to controlled transactions. A Local File is commonly triggered where the aggregate value of a related-party transaction category exceeds EGP 8,000,000 in the relevant fiscal year; the exact threshold, transaction aggregation rules, filing format, and submission deadline must be checked against current ETA regulations and guidance. A Master File generally applies to an Egyptian constituent entity of a multinational enterprise group, and country-by-country reporting generally applies to groups meeting the EUR 750,000,000 consolidated-revenue threshold or the corresponding local-currency test. Separate disclosure and documentation obligations may apply below the Local File threshold.

Economic substance scope: Egypt does not generally operate a standalone United Arab Emirates-style Economic Substance Regulations regime. Nevertheless, commercial substance is relevant under transfer-pricing rules, beneficial-ownership analysis, anti-avoidance principles, treaty interpretation, permanent-establishment analysis, deductibility rules, and the ETA's authority to challenge artificial or non-business arrangements. The LLC should maintain Egyptian people, premises, decision-making, inventory controls, cold-chain operations, commercial risk management, banking activity, customs capability, and records proportionate to its actual wholesale-import function. A conduit entity with no operational capacity may face denial of deductions, treaty benefits, or the asserted tax characterization.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and fraudulent reporting

Statutory sanction: Assessment of unpaid tax, late-payment amounts, administrative penalties, and potential criminal prosecution under Egyptian tax legislation. Fraudulent or repeated conduct can result in substantial monetary penalties and imprisonment, with the sanction depending on the offense, amount, intent, and judicial findings.

Director personal liability: Managers, authorized signatories, finance officers, and other responsible persons may incur criminal liability where they personally participate in, direct, approve, or knowingly facilitate the violation. Civil exposure may include tax restitution and damages. A general travel ban or veil piercing is not automatic; courts and competent authorities may impose personal precautionary or enforcement measures where statutory conditions are met.
Customs undervaluation and import misdeclaration

Statutory sanction: Recovery of customs duty, import taxes, VAT, fines, seizure or confiscation of goods, suspension of customs privileges, and possible criminal proceedings under Egyptian customs legislation. Customs reassessment may also affect deductible cost and transfer-pricing positions.

Director personal liability: Responsible directors, customs representatives, and signatories may face personal criminal or civil exposure for intentional false declarations or customs fraud. Liability is fact-dependent and is not created solely by holding office.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: Primary adjustment of taxable income, denial or limitation of deductions, secondary tax consequences, documentation penalties, interest, and possible audit escalation. A corresponding adjustment is not automatic and may require competent-authority procedures under an applicable treaty.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face personal exposure where they knowingly approve fabricated documentation, sham transactions, or deliberate tax evasion. Ordinary transfer-pricing disagreement generally creates corporate tax exposure rather than automatic personal imprisonment.
Failure to withhold and remit cross-border tax

Statutory sanction: The Egyptian payer may be assessed for the unwithheld tax, related interest or additions, filing penalties, and penalties for inaccurate withholding returns. Contractual gross-up clauses do not eliminate the payer's statutory obligation.

Director personal liability: Authorized signatories and responsible finance personnel may incur personal liability for deliberate failure to withhold, false certificates, or misappropriation of withheld tax. Mere corporate non-payment does not automatically pierce the corporate veil.
VAT and indirect-tax non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Assessment of VAT, denial of input credits, late-filing and late-payment penalties, administrative fines, suspension of tax registration, and criminal exposure for deliberate false invoices or tax fraud.

Director personal liability: Responsible managers and signatories may face personal criminal liability for intentional VAT fraud or false documentation; ordinary VAT assessments ordinarily remain liabilities of the LLC.
AML, sanctions, and illicit-payment risk

Statutory sanction: Suspicious-transaction reporting, account freezing, transaction rejection, regulatory investigation, confiscation, substantial fines, license consequences, and criminal penalties, including imprisonment, under Egyptian AML, anti-corruption, customs, and criminal laws.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally prosecuted for participation, authorization, knowledge, facilitation, obstruction, or failure to establish legally required controls. Asset freezing and travel restrictions may arise under applicable judicial or investigative orders.
Shell company and lack of commercial substance

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty benefits, recharacterization of transactions, disallowance of deductions, transfer-pricing adjustments, withholding assessments, permanent-establishment assertions, and potential anti-evasion or fraudulent-document proceedings where the structure is sham-based.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil restitution and criminal exposure if they knowingly operate a sham entity or submit false records. A lack of substance alone does not automatically create imprisonment or veil piercing; intent, participation, and statutory breach must be established.
Food, veterinary, import, and cold-chain licensing breach

Statutory sanction: Cargo detention, rejection, destruction or re-export, license suspension, administrative fines, product seizure, business closure orders, and possible criminal liability for unsafe, adulterated, or falsely labeled food.

Director personal liability: Directors and operational managers may be personally liable where they knowingly place unsafe or unlawfully imported products on the market. Civil claims may include consumer, public-health, and commercial damages.
Books, records, and beneficial-ownership failure

Statutory sanction: Record-keeping fines, adverse tax estimation, denial of deductions and credits, audit penalties, beneficial-owner disclosure consequences, and escalation where missing records conceal tax or AML offenses.

Director personal liability: The LLC and responsible signatories may be liable for statutory record failures. Personal criminal exposure generally requires proof of intentional destruction, falsification, obstruction, or knowing participation in the underlying offense.
