Competent tax authority

Egyptian Tax Authority (ETA), Egyptian Ministry of Finance. Official portal: https://www.eta.gov.eg; electronic invoicing and clearance portal: https://einvoicing.eta.gov.eg; GAFI one-stop-shop for foreign-invested entities: https://www.gafi.gov.eg.

E-government filing portal

SOP for a foreign-owned EG LLC: (1) Incorporate through GAFI or the Commercial Registry under the Companies Act; (2) obtain the Commercial Register extract; (3) submit the ETA tax-card application (Form 64) with the charter, registered-office proof, managers and beneficial owners identification, and the local manager authorization within 30 days of incorporation; ETA then issues a unified 10-digit TIN and the official Tax Card; (4) register with the ETA VAT directorate once annual or expected taxable supplies exceed EGP 500,000, or voluntarily from EGP 50,000, to obtain the VAT ID and e-invoicing credentials; (5) register as an exporter with Egyptian Customs for physical export operations. Non-resident entities without an Egyptian branch or LLC must appoint a fiscal representative under notarized power of attorney and remain subject to Egyptian withholding on Egyptian-source revenues.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation. The Egyptian LLC is a resident corporate taxperson subject to corporate income tax under Law 91/2005 at the standard rate of 22.5% of worldwide taxable profits; no pass-through or check-the-box classification exists. A foreign branch would instead follow Territorial_Scope rules, being taxed only on Egyptian-source branch profits; the LLC is the sole debtor of VAT and payroll-related obligations.

Available election forms

No check-the-box election exists in Egyptian tax law. Available statutory elections: (i) small and micro enterprise presumptive taxation election under Law 152/2020 for annual turnover up to EGP 20,000,000, replacing accounting-based CIT with 0.5%–1.5% of gross receipts; (ii) group/consolidated tax filing election after ETA pre-clearance where a parent holds at least 90% of shares and voting rights; (iii) voluntary VAT registration below the EGP 500,000 threshold; (iv) Advance Pricing Agreement applications under Egyptian transfer pricing rules for cross-border film licensing and royalty structures; (v) fiscal-year election at first CIT registration. Hybrid mismatch rules are not codified in Egyptian law; general anti-avoidance principles and economic-substance requirements apply.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

14%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

EGP 500,000 of annual taxable supplies within the preceding 12 months triggers mandatory VAT registration (VAT Law 67/2016, Article 17); voluntary registration is available from EGP 50,000 annual turnover; importers must register regardless of turnover. The 2024–2025 tax facilitation package proposes inflation-indexation of the registration threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rate VAT applies to (i) physical exports of goods evidenced by customs exit; (ii) services rendered to, and effectively consumed by, non-residents outside Egypt, including film copyright licensing and royalty revenues when the licensed economic exploitation occurs outside Egypt and consideration is settled in foreign currency. The Egyptian licensor must be VAT-registered, issue a zero-rated electronic invoice, and retain the license contract with foreign-territory clauses, end-beneficiary statements, and foreign-exchange remittance records. Input VAT attributable to zero-rated exports is refundable by cash refund or credit offset.

  • Customs Export Declaration (ACID-validated export manifest released by Egyptian Customs)
  • Bill of Lading or Airway Bill (through-export transport document evidencing physical exit)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (foreign-currency bank credit advice evidencing repatriation to Egypt)

Mandatory for all VAT-registered enterprises. Tax invoices must be submitted in real time to the ETA Integrated E-Invoicing System in IEC XML (UN/CEFACT) format, digitally signed with RSA/SHA-256 credentials, and pass the ETA automated clearance engine before acquiring legal validity; each cleared invoice receives a QR fiscal stamp. B2C sales flow through the separate e-receipts channel. Taxpayers integrate via ETA-approved APIs (OAuth2) or the portal mode; uncleared or rejected invoices are disallowed as input VAT and non-compliance triggers graduated penalties under VAT Law 67/2016.

ETA operates a dedicated export zero-rating audit track; discrepancies in customs exit data, foreign-exchange repatriation, or overseas-use evidence trigger retrospective reversal of the 0% rate, disallowance of input VAT credit, cash-refund clawback, and collection of VAT plus a monthly late-payment surcharge of 1.5% capped at the tax amount. Fraudulent export zero-rating or refund claims attract evasion penalties: imprisonment of at least two years and/or fines of EGP 20,000–100,000 under VAT Law 67/2016, fines of EGP 10,000–50,000 and imprisonment under Income Tax Law 91/2005 Article 133, customs smuggling penalties up to 100% of the export value, loss of GAFI incentives, and blacklisting from the e-invoicing exporter register.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The Egyptian pound (EGP) is domestically convertible for current-account transactions, including trade in goods and services, royalties, film distribution fees, and profit remittance, under a CBE-managed flexible exchange-rate regime. Capital-account transactions are permitted but are documentary and bank-controlled; direct investment, equity injection, shareholder loans, and liquidation proceeds require GAFI registration and CBE-compliant filing before settlement. Profit and dividend repatriation is legally open to foreign owners of an Egyptian LLC, but the practical hurdles are represented by mandatory tax clearance from the Egyptian Tax Authority, audited financial statements, CBE purpose codes, and conversion during periodic U.S. dollar liquidity constraints. Since the 2016 exchange-rate liberalization, the official and market rates have been unified, and no fixed statutory conversion cap applies, but banks may defer conversion in volatile periods while maintaining fair and transparent customer treatment.

Export proceeds surrender rules

For included and service exports, including international film-licensing royalty receipts treated as service export proceeds, the exporter must receive the total proceeds in an Egyptian authorized bank within 90 days from the date of the underlying invoice or contractual due date, unless a CBE-approved extension is granted. The mandatory surrender rule is expressed as 100% repatriation into the Egyptian banking system rather than a prescribed EGP conversion ratio; the proceeds may be held in the exporter's foreign-currency account with an authorized bank and converted to EGP only when required for operational needs or remittance obligations. If export proceeds remain uncollected after the 90-day period, the authorized bank must report the breach to CBE, and the exporter must provide a documentary justification, collection schedule, or legal claim file.

Central bank supervision: Central Bank of Egypt (CBE), General Department for Foreign Transactions, Cairo, in coordination with the General Authority for Investment and Free Zones (GAFI) under the Egyptian Investment Law No. 72 of 2017 and the CBE Banking Law No. 194 of 2020.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Rating: Low for primary treasury purposes. Offshore digital EMIs such as Revolut Business, Wise Business, Mercury, or Payoneer are not licensed by CBE and cannot lawfully function as the principal corporate account of an Egyptian LLC. CBE Banking Law No. 194 of 2020 requires an Egyptian-resident company to direct cross-border receipts through an authorized bank in Egypt, and maintaining an undisclosed or unapproved foreign-currency account abroad is a regulatory violation. Offshore EMIs may be considered only as auxiliary collection tools for small licensing fees when funds are routed onward into the Egyptian banking system through a compliant SWIFT transfer, but local banks and CBE regulators will treat the structure as higher AML/CFT risk unless full documentation of each underlying licence, invoice, and tax record is maintained.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

The compliant routing for the initial foreign investment in the Egyptian LLC is a clean SWIFT MT103 or MT202 wire from the shareholder's foreign bank to the LLC's capital-escrow account at an authorized Egyptian bank. The bank will require the remittance to use the CBE foreign-investment purpose code for direct investment and will require the beneficiary field to state the LLC legal name, commercial registry number, and the underlying GAFI investment file number. Supporting documentation for conversion and transfer to the operating account includes the Arabic-language authenticated articles of association, commercial registry extract, certificate of incorporation, tax card, authorized-signatories register, UBO declarations, and the bank's share-capital release instruction. Where the initial capital is injected in a foreign currency, the Egyptian bank will credit the foreign-currency capital account and convert to EGP only for registration and operating payments where necessary, observing CBE reporting rules.For royalties, profit shares, and film-licensing fees owed to non-resident right holders, the primary clearing rail is SWIFT MT103 from the Egyptian operating account with a CBE-compliant purpose code for royalties or licence fees. The paying bank will require the underlying signed licensing agreement, a commercial invoice, documentary confirmation of the licensed activity, and an Egyptian Tax Authority withholding-tax certificate issued on the gross or net royalty payment. For high-value acquisition or advancement payments for film rights, an irrevocable documentary letter of credit issued via SWIFT MT700 is acceptable, with required documents including the transfer or assignment of copyright certificate, delivery receipt of the licensed master and metadata, scheduled milestone invoice, and production-company authorization; a documentary collection under MT710 may be used for recurring instalments. All outbound EGP conversion must be made through an authorized bank at the prevailing interbank exchange rate and must not involve netting or settlement outside the Egyptian banking system.Spot EGP/USD, EGP/EUR, and EGP/GBP transactions with an authorized Egyptian bank for current-account trade and royalty payments at prevailing interbank market rates.Deliverable onshore EGP foreign-exchange forward contracts with a tenor generally limited to 12 months and supported by a documented current-account exposure, such as an underlying film-licensing royalty or acquisition obligation.Cross-currency swaps executed with a licensed Egyptian bank for hedging principal and interest on shareholder loans or trade-finance facilities, provided the transaction is non-speculative and connected to an underlying commercial cash-flow exposure.Non-deliverable forwards are not generally available onshore for an ordinary Egyptian LLC; any NDF structure must be approved by CBE and is permissible only as a hedge of a real EGP cash-flow obligation, never as a speculative instrument or an offshore arbitrage mechanism.

Offshore digital EMIs such as Revolut Business, Wise Business, Mercury, or Payoneer are not licensed by CBE and cannot lawfully function as the principal corporate account of an Egyptian LLC. CBE Banking Law No. 194 of 2020 requires an Egyptian-resident company to direct cross-border receipts through an authorized bank in Egypt, and maintaining an undisclosed or unapproved foreign-currency account abroad is a regulatory violation. Offshore EMIs may be considered only as auxiliary collection tools for small licensing fees when funds are routed onward into the Egyptian banking system through a compliant SWIFT transfer, but local banks and CBE regulators will treat the structure as higher AML/CFT risk unless full documentation of each underlying licence, invoice, and tax record is maintained.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

22.5% of taxable net profit for ordinary Egyptian companies, including limited liability companies, under the Egyptian Income Tax Law.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

10% generally applies to dividends distributed by an Egyptian resident company. A 5% rate may apply to qualifying distributions relating to listed shares or other statutory conditions. The applicable tax base, exemptions, listed-company conditions, and treaty rate must be tested at the distribution date. Treaty rates vary by recipient residence and treaty provisions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The annual return and tax payment are generally due within four months after the end of the fiscal year. For a calendar-year company, the usual deadline is 30 April of the following year. A company using a non-calendar fiscal year applies the same four-month rule to its approved fiscal year-end.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Withholding tax must generally be deducted at source when the payment is made, credited, or otherwise becomes due under the applicable rule, and reported and remitted within the prescribed statutory period. The payment-specific deadline must be confirmed from the current Egyptian Tax Authority instructions.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and fraudulent reporting

Statutory sanction: Assessment of unpaid tax, late-payment charges, administrative penalties, possible confiscation or seizure measures, and criminal prosecution. Deliberate tax evasion under Egyptian tax legislation may carry imprisonment generally ranging from six months to five years and a fine or alternative penalty calculated under the applicable statute, with aggravated consequences for repeated or organized conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors, managers, authorized signatories, and persons who deliberately participate may face personal criminal liability and civil recovery. Corporate limited liability does not protect an individual from liability for personal wrongdoing. Travel restrictions or asset restraints may be imposed by competent authorities or courts where legally authorized; veil piercing is not automatic.
Failure to withhold or remit non-resident tax

Statutory sanction: Recovery of the tax from the Egyptian LLC, late-payment charges, statutory filing penalties, denial of treaty relief, and possible criminal consequences for deliberate non-compliance or falsified withholding records.

Director personal liability: Responsible officers may incur personal liability where they authorized or knowingly facilitated the failure. A director is not automatically liable solely because of office, but personal participation, gross misconduct, or statutory responsible-person provisions can defeat the limited-liability protection.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: Primary adjustment to arm's-length income, secondary tax exposure where applicable, denial of deductions, additional tax, late-payment charges, documentation penalties, and potential audit extension or dispute proceedings.

Director personal liability: Personal liability normally requires evidence of intentional misconduct, fraud, or a specific statutory breach. Directors may face civil recovery and criminal exposure if they knowingly approved fabricated documentation or fraudulent profit shifting.
Permanent-establishment and source-tax avoidance

Statutory sanction: Recharacterization of income, assessment of Egyptian corporate income tax, withholding tax, penalties, interest or equivalent charges, and potential treaty dispute or anti-abuse consequences.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior managers may face personal exposure for deliberate misrepresentation or obstruction. Ordinary business failure or an incorrect good-faith technical position does not, by itself, create automatic personal criminal liability.
Unlicensed or unauthorized copyright exploitation

Statutory sanction: Civil injunctions, damages, account of profits, seizure or destruction of infringing copies, administrative measures, and criminal copyright sanctions under Egyptian intellectual-property legislation, depending on the conduct.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly authorize or participate in infringement may face personal criminal and civil liability. Corporate status does not protect a person who personally commits or directs the infringement.
Anti-money laundering and suspicious-payment conduct

Statutory sanction: Suspicious transaction reporting, account freezing or transaction restriction, confiscation, administrative sanctions, and criminal penalties under Egyptian anti-money-laundering legislation, potentially including imprisonment and fines linked to the relevant offense or proceeds.

Director personal liability: Directors, compliance officers, beneficial owners, and authorized signatories may face personal liability for knowing participation, facilitation, concealment, or failure to comply with applicable obligations. Travel bans and asset freezes require competent-authority or judicial action and are not automatic.
Beneficial-ownership and corporate-record failure

Statutory sanction: Registration or tax penalties, enhanced due-diligence restrictions, account suspension, investigation, tax reassessment, and criminal sanctions where concealment supports fraud, money laundering, or another offense.

Director personal liability: Managers and signatories may be liable for knowingly false corporate records or declarations. Limited liability does not protect deliberate concealment or misuse of the company.
Books, records, and electronic-filing failure

Statutory sanction: Record-keeping penalties, presumptive or estimated assessment, denial of deductions, adverse evidentiary inferences, late-filing penalties, and additional tax and late-payment charges.

Director personal liability: The company bears the primary compliance liability, but responsible managers may be personally sanctioned for deliberate obstruction, destruction of records, false statements, or knowingly approving non-compliant filings.
