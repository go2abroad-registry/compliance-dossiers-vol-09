Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Bundesministerium der Finanzen (BMF) as federal policy authority; Bundeszentralamt für Steuern (BZSt) for issuance and administration of Umsatzsteuer-Identifikationsnummer (USt-IdNr.); local Finanzamt for tax registration, assessment and collection; ELSTER (Elektronische Steuererklärung) as the mandatory electronic filing portal; Generalzolldirektion (GZD) and local Hauptzollamt for customs and import VAT administration; ATLAS system for electronic customs declarations.

E-government filing portal

Foreign entities without a German seat that make taxable supplies in Germany must register with the competent local Finanzamt, normally by filing the Fragebogen zur steuerlichen Erfassung via ELSTER or in paper form; a Steuernummer is issued for VAT return filing. A USt-IdNr. is separately obtained from the BZSt for intra-EU and cross-border VAT identification. Non-EU established taxable persons may be required to appoint a German fiscal representative under §§22a-22f UStG unless a simplified or exemption procedure applies; EU-established entities may register directly. For customs brokerage, the entity must obtain an EORI number from the Generalzolldirektion and register in ATLAS for customs declarations. German VAT returns are filed electronically via ELSTER; customs and import VAT declarations are electronic via ATLAS.

Available election forms

No US-style check-the-box election and no disregarded entity election are available for a UG. Available mechanisms include: VAT group treatment (umsatzsteuerliche Organschaft) under §2(2) UStG; corporate income tax group (Organschaft) under §§14-19 KStG; trade tax group under §2(2) GewStG; participation exemption for dividends and capital gains under §8b KStG; and anti-hybrid mismatch rules under §§4k and 4l EStG. No hybrid entity or hybrid mismatch election exists in German domestic law; EU ATAD anti-hybrid rules apply directly through these provisions.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

0% VAT applies to exports of goods to non-EU countries under §4 Nr. 1a and §6 UStG where the exporter proves that the goods have left the EU customs territory. Intra-EU supplies are zero-rated under §4 Nr. 1b and §6a UStG if the transport or dispatch to another Member State and the acquirer's VAT identification are substantiated. B2B services, including customs brokerage services, supplied to foreign taxable persons outside Germany are generally outside the scope of German VAT or subject to reverse charge under §3a(2) UStG; ancillary services may follow the principal supply. Refunds for non-established businesses may be claimed under §18 UStG or the EU VAT refund procedure under Directive 2008/9/EC.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The euro is fully convertible in Germany. Both current and capital account operations are unrestricted under Section 106 of the German Foreign Trade and Payments Ordinance (Außenwirtschaftsverordnung, AWV) and Article 63 TFEU on the free movement of capital. There are no exchange controls, no prior authorization requirements for dividend remittances, capital repayments, or trade payments, and no distinction in enforcement between resident and non-resident shareholders. Profit remittance hurdles are limited to corporate and tax law: a UG must retain 25% of annual surplus under Section 5a of the German Limited Liability Companies Act (GmbHG) until subscribed capital reaches EUR 25,000; distributions to non-resident shareholders are subject to German withholding tax on dividends (25% plus 5.5% solidarity surcharge, i.e. 26.375%, reduced or exempt under applicable double-tax treaties). The only FX-related compliance obligation is statistical reporting to the Deutsche Bundesbank for any cross-border payment or receipt of EUR 12,500 or more.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Germany imposes no export proceeds surrender requirement and no mandatory conversion ratio. Export receipts and customs-brokerage commissions denominated in foreign currency may be held offshore or onshore indefinitely. The sole regulatory duty is the AWV reporting obligation: cross-border receipts and payments equal to or exceeding EUR 12,500 must be reported to the Deutsche Bundesbank for balance-of-payments statistics (Zahlungsbilanzstatistik) within the applicable monthly reporting cycle, ordinarily through the customer's German bank or directly by the resident customs broker where the broker is the reporting party. Payments classified as customs duties, brokerage fees, or transit guarantees must be annotated with the correct statistical purpose code to avoid misclassification.

Central bank supervision: Deutsche Bundesbank (Frankfurt am Main) is the German central bank and external-payments statistical authority within the Eurosystem of the European Central Bank (ECB). BaFin (Bundesanstalt für Finanzdienstleistungsaufsicht) supervises German banks, payment institutions, and e-money institutions under the KWG and ZAG. The Generalzolldirektion (German Federal Customs Authority) administers customs duty collection and AEO/EORI licensing for customs brokers.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Revolut Fit: High

Feasibility rating: MEDIUM with material legal constraints. Fully licensed EEA e-money institutions or payment banks, such as N26 Bank GmbH (full German banking license under KWG) or Revolut Bank UAB (European Central Bank license passported into Germany via a German branch), can operate liquid EUR accounts, provide IBAN collection infrastructure, and execute SEPA and SWIFT transfers for the UG. However, a customs brokerage classified as Medium_Risk_Enhanced faces: (i) automatic enhanced-due-diligence review queues, delaying onboarding by 10 to 20 business days; (ii) statutory inability of EMIs to extend credit, provide FX derivatives, or maintain a formal capital escrow account because those activities require a full CRR/CRD IV banking license; (iii) practical rejection or manual intervention for large inbound customs-duty prepayments originating from non-EEA principals, due to the absence of physical branch relationship management; and (iv) uneven access to SWIFT MT103 priority processing, as many EMI firms route through partner banks. The mandatory UG capital contribution and any custodial duty-collection balances must therefore sit in a CRR-licensed German bank account; EMI rails are viable only as a secondary disbursement channel.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% corporate income tax under the Körperschaftsteuergesetz, plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on the corporate income tax, producing an effective federal corporate tax burden of 15.825%. Trade tax is additionally imposed by the municipality; the combined corporate and trade tax burden commonly approximates 29.8% to 33%, depending on the municipal multiplier.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% German capital-yield withholding tax plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on that withholding tax, producing an aggregate domestic deduction of 26.375%, before treaty or statutory exemption relief. A qualifying corporate shareholder may obtain relief under the German participation exemption, the EU Parent-Subsidiary framework as implemented in Germany, or an applicable tax treaty, subject to minimum holding, anti-abuse, beneficial-ownership, substance, and procedural conditions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The annual corporate income tax and trade tax returns are generally due by 31 July of the following calendar year. Where a tax adviser is engaged, the extended statutory deadline generally runs to the last day of February of the second following year, subject to statutory exceptions and special administrative extensions. The tax authority may require an earlier filing. Corporate income tax prepayments are generally due on 10 March, 10 June, 10 September, and 10 December. Trade tax prepayments are generally due on 15 February, 15 May, 15 August, and 15 November.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: German transfer-pricing documentation is generally required for cross-border controlled transactions upon request, with special transaction thresholds under the Transfer Pricing Documentation Ordinance generally at EUR 6 million for supplies of goods and EUR 600,000 for other transactions, including services, financing, and licensing. A master file is generally required for a German entity belonging to a multinational enterprise group where the group's consolidated revenue exceeded EUR 100 million in the preceding fiscal year. A country-by-country report generally applies to qualifying multinational groups with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million. Documentation must be prepared within the statutory response period when requested; material non-compliance can lead to adverse estimation and a surcharge generally ranging from 5% to 10% of the estimated income adjustment, subject to statutory rules.

Economic substance scope: Germany does not operate a single general regime formally designated as Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to certain low-tax jurisdictions. Substance is nevertheless material under the arm's-length principle, German anti-abuse rule in section 42 AO, treaty anti-abuse provisions, section 50d(3) EStG, CFC rules, hybrid-mismatch rules, interest limitation rules, and beneficial-ownership requirements. A German UG conducting customs brokerage should demonstrate real German management, qualified personnel, operational premises or credible infrastructure, customs authorisations, decision-making capacity, segregation of client funds where relevant, commercial risk assumption, books and records, and actual performance of brokerage and transit-management functions. A conduit company with no personnel, control, decision-making, or operational capability may lose deductions, treaty benefits, or exemption status and may face income reallocation.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Intentional tax evasion may result in a criminal fine or imprisonment of up to five years; particularly serious cases may result in imprisonment from six months to ten years. Tax must be reassessed with interest, and administrative penalties, confiscation, and professional or licensing consequences may apply.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may incur personal civil liability under section 69 AO for tax claims, interest, and related financial consequences caused intentionally or through gross negligence by breach of tax duties. Criminal liability may attach for personal participation, ordering, aiding, or approving the conduct. Travel bans and imprisonment are not automatic consequences of ordinary tax arrears; coercive measures, asset seizure, detention, or travel restrictions require statutory grounds and competent-authority action.
Customs Fraud and Import-VAT Evasion

Statutory sanction: Customs debt, import VAT, default interest, administrative fines, seizure, forfeiture, loss of customs simplifications, and criminal proceedings may apply under the Union Customs Code, German customs legislation, and tax-criminal law. Serious intentional conduct may carry imprisonment, particularly where organised or commercially systematic.

Director personal liability: Directors and customs representatives may be personally liable where they intentionally or negligently breach customs obligations or knowingly facilitate false declarations. Customs authorisations, broker access, and trusted-trader privileges may be suspended or revoked.
Unauthorised Customs Representation

Statutory sanction: Customs declarations may be rejected or reassessed; the representative may become liable for customs debt, duties, import VAT, penalties, and loss of authorisations. Fraudulent representation may constitute a criminal offence.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal liability for organisational failures, inadequate controls, or knowingly unauthorised representation. The corporate form does not protect a director from liability for personal misconduct or statutory duty breaches.
Money Laundering and Terrorist-Financing Controls

Statutory sanction: Violations of the German Anti-Money Laundering Act may result in administrative fines, supervisory measures, reporting restrictions, reputational consequences, and criminal liability for laundering or terrorist-financing conduct. Serious money laundering may result in imprisonment, with enhanced exposure for organised or commercial activity.

Director personal liability: Managing directors and designated AML-responsible persons may be personally liable for intentional participation or negligent organisational failures. Directors must establish risk assessment, customer due diligence, beneficial-owner verification, record retention, transaction monitoring, escalation, and suspicious-activity reporting procedures.
Sanctions, Export-Control, and Dual-Use Violations

Statutory sanction: German and European sanctions and export-control violations may result in substantial administrative fines, criminal prosecution, confiscation, transaction prohibitions, licence revocation, and imprisonment, depending on the conduct and applicable regime.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable where they knowingly participate or fail to establish reasonable compliance controls. A foreign parent's instruction does not excuse a German director from German and EU sanctions obligations.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation and Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: The Finanzamt may increase German taxable income, deny deductions, impose interest, estimate the tax base, impose documentation surcharges, and apply withholding-tax consequences. Deliberate misstatements may constitute tax evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for tax losses caused by knowingly approving non-arm's-length arrangements, false documentation, or failure to preserve records. The absence of a Local File does not itself prove evasion but materially increases audit and estimation risk.
Treaty Abuse and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: Treaty or EU relief may be denied, German withholding tax may be assessed retroactively with interest, and the payer may be liable for underwithheld tax. Anti-treaty-shopping rules and general anti-abuse provisions may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal liability for knowingly approving unsupported exemption certificates, failing to verify recipient entitlement, or making distributions without withholding where required.
Corporate Insolvency and Tax-Payment Priority Breach

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include director liability, clawback of transactions, fines, imprisonment for insolvency offences, tax recovery, interest, and disqualification from management. German insolvency filing duties and timing must be assessed immediately upon insolvency or over-indebtedness.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for prohibited payments after insolvency, unpaid taxes and social-security contributions, delayed insolvency filing, and losses caused to creditors. The UG's limited liability does not shield directors from statutory management liability.
Payroll, Social-Security, and Employment Tax Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Back taxes, social-security assessments, interest, late-payment charges, administrative fines, criminal prosecution, and exclusion from public contracts may apply. Deliberate non-payment of employee social-security contributions is particularly serious.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for withheld but unpaid wage taxes, employee social-security contributions, and intentional or grossly negligent compliance failures. Criminal exposure may arise from withholding and diversion of employee contributions.
Shell Company, Nominee Management, and Corporate-Register Misuse

Statutory sanction: Possible consequences include tax assessments, denial of deductions and treaty benefits, register and licensing measures, fines, criminal prosecution for fraud or tax evasion, asset seizure, and insolvency proceedings.

Director personal liability: Nominee and de facto directors may be treated as responsible participants where they exercise management or knowingly facilitate unlawful conduct. Corporate limited liability does not protect against personal tort, tax, insolvency, fraud, or criminal liability. Travel restrictions or imprisonment require specific statutory and procedural grounds and are not automatic.
