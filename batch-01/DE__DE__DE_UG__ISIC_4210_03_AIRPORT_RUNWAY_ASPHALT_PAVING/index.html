Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Federal Central Tax Office (Bundeszentralamt für Steuern - BZSt) for the VAT identification number (USt-IdNr.) and the local tax office (Finanzamt) for corporate income tax (Körperschaftsteuer), trade tax (Gewerbesteuer) and VAT assessment in each German Land; electronic filing and official communication through the ELSTER portal (www.elster.de).

E-government filing portal

A non-resident-owned UG obtains its statutory tax number (Steuernummer) and VAT ID in a three-step procedure: (1) notarize the articles of association, register the UG in the Commercial Register (Handelsregister) at the local court (Amtsgericht), and appoint a German-resident managing director (Geschäftsführer), while the UG must maintain its registered office in Germany; (2) file the tax registration questionnaire ('Fragebogen zur steuerlichen Erfassung', form USt 1 A) with the local Finanzamt within one month of incorporation, disclosing the intended business scope (ISIC 4210 / NAICS 237310 runway asphalt paving), the managing director, shareholders, and bank accounts; the Finanzamt then assigns the corporate income tax and trade tax account number; (3) apply separately to the BZSt for the USt-IdNr. where intra-Community or export activities exist; non-resident shareholders do not obtain a German TIN for the UG itself, but foreign contractor-partners must evidence their foreign VAT registration for the reverse-charge mechanism under Section 13b UStG.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

Germany has no US-style check-the-box election. A UG (haftungsbeschränkt) is mandatorily taxable at entity level: corporate income tax at 15% plus 5.5% solidarity surcharge (effective 15.825%) and trade tax (Gewerbesteuer) at a municipal rate of approximately 14-17%, with no pass-through option for income tax or VAT. Available statutory elections relevant to this UG include: (1) Organschaft tax group election (Secs. 14, 17 KStG; Sec. 2(2) No. 2 GewStG; Sec. 2(2) No. 2 UStG) where a German tax-resident parent controls at least 50% of the voting rights and integrates the UG into a single corporate income tax, trade tax and VAT group; (2) the small-entrepreneur (Kleinunternehmer) waiver under Section 19 UStG, subject to the statutory turnover ceilings now aligned with EU Directive 2020/285 (approx. EUR 25,000 prior-year and EUR 100,000 current-year revenue), which is factually unavailable for a large civil-aviation runway contract but remains a formal election if revenues are low; (3) hybrid mismatch recharacterization elections under Section 4k EStG (ATAD implementation) to neutralize double non-taxation vis-à-vis a foreign shareholder; none of these converts the UG into a pass-through entity for German tax purposes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

19%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

Germany applies no turnover threshold for resident entrepreneurs: the UG must register for VAT and file the registration questionnaire from the first taxable transaction. Non-resident entrepreneurs are likewise registrable without any minimum threshold from the first taxable supply in Germany. A small-business optional exemption exists under Sec. 19 UStG (EUR 25,000 previous-year turnover and EUR 100,000 current-year ceiling as transposed for 2025), but a UG executing airport-runway asphalt paving contracts will ordinarily exceed the ceiling and must charge 19% VAT (7% reduced rate is not available for construction services) on all domestic taxable supplies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Export zero-rating is available under Sec. 6 UStG for goods physically exported from the EU customs territory to a third country, under Sec. 6a UStG for intra-Community supplies to a customer holding a valid USt-IdNr., and under Sec. 8 UStG for services performed on movable tangible property that is subsequently exported. For the UG's core pavement contracts, an airport runway is immovable property: the place of supply under Sec. 3a(2) UStG is the location of the runway, so asphalt paving performed at a German airport is a domestic supply chargeable at 19% and cannot be zero-rated. Zero-rating may be claimed only for separable and documented transactions such as the export dispatch of polymer-modified bitumen binders, specialized paving equipment, or mobile machinery to non-EU airport project sites, provided the goods leave the EU customs territory and the exporter obtains the required proof of export (Sec. 6(3) UStG) within the statutory documentation period. Services rendered abroad for non-EU airport sites fall outside German VAT where the immovable-property place-of-supply rule localizes them outside Germany.

  • Customs Export Declaration (ATLAS electronic export declaration, including the EXA summary declaration and movement reference number evidencing exit of the goods from the EU customs territory)
  • Bill of Lading / Air Waybill / CMR consignment note (transport document identifying the consignor, the non-EU airport project site, and the actual physical export movement)
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or equivalent bank credit confirmation (SWIFT MT103) evidencing receipt of the aggregate export consideration in the UG's German bank account

Germany does not currently operate a real-time fiscal clearance or digital stamping regime (no Government-to-Business API clearance as in the SdI model). Pursuant to the Growth Opportunities Act (Wachstumschancengesetz) and EU Directive 2025/2054 (VAT in the Digital Age - ViDA), mandatory B2B e-invoicing applies from 1 January 2027: all invoices must be issued and transmitted in a structured XML format meeting the CEN EN 16931 semantic model (XRechnung, ZUGFeRD 2.0.1 including the Factur-X / Cross-Industry Invoice (CII) profile), with an obligation on all German recipients to accept e-invoices; B2C e-invoicing and transaction data reporting to a central digital platform follow in 2028/2030. Until 31 December 2026, paper, PDF, EDI and hybrid ZUGFeRD invoices remain legally receivable; the UG's ERP must be adapted to support receipt and archiving of e-invoices from 2027. No governmental approval, API clearance, or fiscal stamp is required for individual documents in Germany.

Export zero-rating is examined through the German customs administration (Zoll) integrated import/export records and the Finanzamt's external audit (Außenprüfung). Missing or defective proof of export pursuant to Sec. 6(3) UStG and the Belegnachweis regulation leads to retrospective VAT assessment at 19%, statutory default interest under Secs. 233a and 238 AO, and denial of input VAT deduction to the customer. Issuance of an invoice showing VAT or an export exemption without entitlement triggers liability under Sec. 14c UStG (incorrect VAT amount shown) and joint-and-several liability of the contractor under Sec. 25d UStG for VAT evaded by a subcontractor. Fraudulent export zero-rating or refund claims constitute tax evasion under Sec. 370 AO (imprisonment up to 5 years, up to 10 years in especially serious cases) and may be fined under Sec. 26a UStG and Sec. 375 AO; any refund obtained is clawed back with interest, and assets may be seized under Sec. 12a AO. For refundable export schemes the 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation covenant is imposed as a refund-condition, with default triggering partial or full refund revocation and penalty interest.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Germany is part of the euro area and has no general exchange-control approval regime for ordinary current-account or capital-account transactions. The euro is freely convertible. Cross-border investment, capital contributions, loans, dividends, and trade payments generally do not require prior Bundesbank approval, subject to sanctions, anti-money-laundering controls, tax rules, corporate-law requirements, and applicable statistical reporting. Dividend remittances by a UG require distributable profits, duly adopted accounts and shareholder resolutions, compliance with the UG’s statutory reserve obligation under section 5a of the GmbH Act, and withholding-tax procedures. Treaty relief or a refund may be available for nonresident shareholders, subject to eligibility and documentation. Interest, royalties, and service payments require appropriate contractual and tax substantiation; withholding tax may apply depending on payment type and recipient.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Germany imposes no general requirement to repatriate or surrender export proceeds and no mandatory conversion ratio. Export proceeds may be retained in foreign currency or held abroad, subject to applicable bank, sanctions, tax, accounting, and statistical reporting obligations. Under AWV section 67, resident persons generally report qualifying cross-border payments to or from nonresidents when the applicable reporting threshold is exceeded; since January 2025 the threshold is generally more than EUR 50,000 per payment, subject to statutory exemptions and current Bundesbank instructions. Reporting is statistical, not an approval or surrender requirement. The company should confirm current reporting scope, exemptions, deadlines, and submission method with the Bundesbank or its bank for each payment category.

Central bank supervision: Deutsche Bundesbank is Germany’s central bank and administers balance-of-payments reporting under the Foreign Trade and Payments Act (Außenwirtschaftsgesetz, AWG) and Foreign Trade and Payments Ordinance (Außenwirtschaftsverordnung, AWV). The European Central Bank (ECB) sets euro-area monetary policy. BaFin supervises German credit institutions and financial services providers; it does not administer routine FX approvals.

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% corporation tax under the Körperschaftsteuergesetz, plus a solidarity surcharge of 5.5% on the corporation tax, producing an aggregate federal corporation-tax burden of 15.825%. German trade tax is imposed separately by the municipality; the combined corporation-tax, solidarity-surcharge, and trade-tax burden is commonly approximately 23% to 33%, depending on the municipal multiplier.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% German withholding tax plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on the withholding tax, resulting in an effective domestic withholding rate of 26.375% on dividends and similar profit distributions, subject to exemptions and treaty relief. A German corporation distributing dividends to a qualifying EU parent may obtain exemption or refund relief under the German implementation of the EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive, generally requiring at least 10% direct participation, a minimum one-year holding period for the participation exemption, beneficial ownership, and anti-abuse compliance. Domestic German participation-exemption rules and treaty relief must be analysed separately.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The annual corporation-tax and trade-tax returns are generally due seven months after the end of the fiscal year. For a calendar-year company without a tax adviser, the normal deadline is 31 July of the following year. Where a tax adviser prepares the return, the general extended deadline is the last day of February of the second following year, subject to statutory transitional rules, tax-office requests, and anti-abuse limitations. Advance corporation-tax and trade-tax payments are generally due on 10 March, 10 June, 10 September, and 10 December.

Deadline: · Frequency:

VAT advance returns and payments are generally due by the tenth day after the end of the filing period. A permanent filing extension may be available; monthly filers generally need to make a special advance payment. The annual VAT return is generally due seven months after the end of the calendar year, subject to applicable adviser extensions and tax-office requirements.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Germany does not require a single transaction-value threshold before the general cross-border related-party documentation duty arises. Under section 90(3) AO and the German Profit Attribution Documentation Ordinance, material cross-border related-party transactions require transaction-specific documentation. A Local File is generally triggered for annual transactions exceeding EUR 6 million for supplies of goods or EUR 600,000 for other transactions, including many services, financing, and royalty transactions. A Master File is generally required for German entities belonging to a multinational group with consolidated annual revenue of at least EUR 100 million. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to multinational groups meeting the EUR 750 million consolidated-revenue threshold.

Economic substance scope: Germany has no separate, universally applicable Economic Substance Regulation regime equivalent to certain low-tax jurisdictions. Substance remains decisive under section 42 AO general anti-abuse rules, treaty beneficial-ownership and anti-conduit principles, the EU anti-avoidance framework, CFC rules, hybrid-mismatch rules, interest-limitation rules, and permanent-establishment analysis. The UG should maintain German commercial premises where appropriate, capable management and decision-making, personnel or operational capacity proportionate to the runway-paving business, construction equipment and project records, independent business rationale, bank-account control, and contemporaneous evidence of services. A foreign parent or conduit company that merely receives dividends, royalties, interest, or service fees without operational substance is exposed to denial of treaty or directive relief and recharacterisation of payments.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and fraudulent tax returns

Statutory sanction: Intentional tax evasion is punishable under section 370 AO by imprisonment of up to five years or a fine. Particularly serious cases may carry imprisonment from six months to ten years. Tax, interest, late-payment charges, and administrative recovery remain payable.

Director personal liability: Managing directors can face criminal prosecution, personal assessment for unpaid taxes caused by intentional or grossly negligent breach of tax duties under section 69 AO, confiscation of proceeds, professional disqualification, and civil claims. The corporate veil is not automatically pierced, but personal liability can arise directly from statutory breach.
Withholding-tax failure

Statutory sanction: The UG may be assessed for the unwithheld tax, solidarity surcharge, interest, and penalties. Intentional or reckless failure may constitute a tax offence or tax administrative offence.

Director personal liability: Directors responsible for payment controls may be personally liable under section 69 AO where culpable breach caused the withholding-tax loss. A treaty certificate alone is insufficient if beneficial ownership or anti-abuse conditions are not satisfied.
VAT fraud and construction reverse-charge failure

Statutory sanction: Additional VAT, interest, late-payment surcharges, audit assessments, invoice-correction obligations, and criminal or administrative sanctions may apply. Intentional conduct may constitute tax evasion.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur personal tax liability and criminal exposure where they knowingly or negligently approved incorrect VAT reporting, failed to maintain invoice controls, or continued filing after detecting irregularities.
Transfer-pricing manipulation and hidden profit distribution

Statutory sanction: The tax authority may increase taxable income, deny deductions, recharacterise payments as hidden profit distributions, impose withholding tax, charge interest, apply documentation surcharges, and impose penalties for inaccurate returns.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for tax losses caused by knowingly approving non-arm's-length arrangements and may face breach-of-duty claims from the UG, shareholders, insolvency administrators, or creditors.
Permanent-establishment and construction-site omission

Statutory sanction: Germany may tax attributable profits, impose corporation tax, trade tax, VAT, payroll tax, interest, penalties, and registration obligations retroactively. Treaty construction-site thresholds must be tested under the specific treaty and facts.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for unpaid payroll, VAT, withholding, and other taxes where they failed to investigate project presence or ignored warning signs.
Illegal distribution and UG capital-maintenance breach

Statutory sanction: Invalid distributions may require repayment and may trigger claims under the German GmbH Act, insolvency law, and civil law. Tax recharacterisation as a hidden profit distribution may produce additional corporation tax and withholding tax.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may face repayment claims, damages, insolvency-related personal liability, disqualification, and criminal exposure for prohibited payments after insolvency maturity.
Late filing or defective filing of insolvency

Statutory sanction: Failure to file insolvency without undue delay can carry criminal sanctions, repayment claims, director-liability assessments, and insolvency-estate recovery actions. Tax enforcement and payment restrictions may continue.

Director personal liability: Directors can face imprisonment or fines under insolvency law, personal compensation claims for prohibited payments, tax liability under section 69 AO, and civil claims by the insolvency administrator.
Money laundering, beneficial-ownership, and AML failure

Statutory sanction: Breaches of the German Money Laundering Act may result in significant administrative fines, reporting restrictions, enhanced supervision, account termination, confiscation, and criminal liability where money laundering or predicate offences are involved.

Director personal liability: Directors and compliance-responsible persons may face personal fines, criminal prosecution, confiscation, management bans, and liability for failing to implement risk-based controls.
Corruption and public-procurement misconduct

Statutory sanction: German criminal law may impose imprisonment and fines for bribery and commercial corruption. Contracts may be terminated, proceeds confiscated, procurement exclusion imposed, and corporate administrative fines levied under the German Administrative Offences Act.

Director personal liability: Directors, employees, and intermediaries may be personally prosecuted, fined, imprisoned, disqualified, and subjected to confiscation. The company may face damages, debarment, and reputational exclusion from airport and public projects.
Unlicensed aviation, construction, or environmental operations

Statutory sanction: Authorities may impose stop-work orders, administrative fines, remediation and removal obligations, permit revocation, contractual damages, and criminal sanctions for serious safety or environmental violations.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal fines, criminal liability for negligent endangerment or environmental offences, civil damages, and claims for project delay, remediation, and personal injury.
Payroll tax and social-security non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Back taxes, social-security contributions, interest, fines, worksite inspections, payment demands, and criminal sanctions for withholding and social-security fraud may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors can be personally liable for unpaid employee social-security contributions, wage-tax failures, and intentional or negligent breaches, with potential imprisonment or fines.
Shell company and substance failure

Statutory sanction: Authorities may deny treaty and directive benefits, disregard deductions, recharacterise payments, impose withholding tax and penalties, apply section 42 AO anti-abuse principles, and challenge the asserted tax residence or permanent-establishment position.

Director personal liability: Directors may face tax liability, civil damages, confiscation, insolvency claims, and criminal exposure where the entity was used knowingly for fraud, evasion, money laundering, or creditor prejudice.
