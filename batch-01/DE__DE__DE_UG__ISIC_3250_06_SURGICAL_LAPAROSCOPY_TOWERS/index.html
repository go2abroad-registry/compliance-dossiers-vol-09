Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Bundeszentralamt für Steuern (BZSt - Federal Central Tax Office) issues the German VAT ID (USt-IdNr) and handles central tax functions; the competent local Finanzamt (district tax office) administers German corporate income tax, trade tax, VAT returns, payroll tax and tax audits. National e-filing portals: ELSTER (www.elster.de) and BZSt Online-Portal (www.bzst.de).

E-government filing portal

1. Constitute the UG before a German notary, execute the articles of association, appoint a managing director, and register the company in the Commercial Register (Handelsregister) of the local court (Amtsgericht). 2. Open a German business bank account for the UG and pay in the required formation capital (minimum EUR 1; mandatory annual retention of 25% of annual profit until the ordinary EUR 25,000 GmbH minimum capital is reached). 3. Within one month of business commencement, file the 'Fragebogen zur steuerlichen Erfassung' (statutory tax registration questionnaire) with the local Finanzamt via ELSTER, disclosing shareholders, business activities, accounting date and expected turnover. 4. The Finanzamt assigns the national tax number (Steuernummer) and issues corporation tax, trade tax and VAT advance assessments. 5. Separately apply to BZSt for the German VAT identification number (USt-IdNr, format DE + 9 digits) for intra-EU supplies and third-country exports; BZSt issues the VAT ID typically within five business days. Non-resident shareholders may hold 100% of the UG; at least one managing director must reside in Germany or another EU/EEA state, or a German resident must be named as authorized recipient of service.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation - A German UG (Unternehmergesellschaft haftungsbeschraenkt) is a resident corporation with place of management or seat in Germany and is subject to unlimited corporate income tax and trade tax on worldwide income. Headline tax burden: 15% corporate income tax plus 5.5% solidarity surcharge (0.825%) plus municipal trade tax (Gewerbesteuer) at an effective rate of 7% to 17.15% depending on the local Hebesatz, producing an aggregate tax burden of approximately 30% to 33% on trading profits. Dividend distributions are further subject to 26.375% withholding tax (25% capital gains tax plus solidarity surcharge) unless reduced under an applicable tax treaty or the EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive. The UG is not eligible for pass-through taxation in Germany.

Available election forms

Germany has no domestic check-the-box or general pass-through election; the UG is a per-se corporate taxpayer. Available elections and structuring mechanisms: (a) Organschaft fiscal unity election under Secs. 14-19 KStG, Sec. 2(2) GewStG and Sec. 2(2)(2) UStG, consolidating the UG with a qualifying German parent for corporate income tax, trade tax and VAT, subject to >50% financial integration, a profit-and-loss transfer agreement (Beherrschungs- und Gewinnabfuehrungsvertrag) and registration in the Commercial Register; (b) waiver of the small-business VAT exemption under Sec. 19(2) UStG to recover input VAT where turnover is below the statutory threshold; (c) European OSS/MOSS one-stop-shop VAT registration elections for distance sales; (d) hybrid mismatch elections under Sec. 4k EStG (ATAD implementation) relevant where the UG functions as a hybrid or reverse-hybrid entity within a foreign-owned cross-border structure.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

19%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

EUR 22,000 (prior calendar year turnover) and EUR 50,000 (current-year projection) under Sec. 19 UStG through 31 December 2024; from 1 January 2025 the thresholds increase to EUR 25,000 (prior year) and EUR 100,000 (current year). A UG manufacturing and exporting surgical laparoscopy towers will normally exceed these thresholds, making VAT registration mandatory. Non-resident entrepreneurs are registrable with no threshold where the place of supply is Germany and reverse-charge does not shift the tax liability.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero-rating requires an export delivery (Ausfuhrlieferung) under Sec. 4 No. 1a in conjunction with Sec. 6 UStG: (i) the UG, or a commissioned carrier or consignee, dispatches or transports the laparoscopy towers and rigid endoscopes from Germany to a third country outside the EU or to a free zone; (ii) the UG is the exporter of record under the Union Customs Code; (iii) the goods physically leave the EU customs territory; and (iv) the UG holds contemporaneous, plausible export proof (Ausfuhrnachweis per Secs. 8-13 UStDV) comprising the customs declaration and transport evidence. For linked offshore services supplied to third-country end users (repair, technical documentation, training for surgical laparoscopy towers), zero-rating also applies where the place of supply is outside the EU under Sec. 3a(2) UStG. Supplies to EU hospitals are zero-rated only as intra-Community supplies under Sec. 4 No. 1b UStG, subject to a valid EU VAT ID of the customer and monthly VIES recapitulative statements. German law imposes no statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline due to EU free movement of capital; the 180-day benchmark reflects the commercial documentary standard applied by export credit insurers and German tax auditors to evidence collection of export consideration.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Ausfuhranmeldung) lodged in ATLAS, with IE-529 validation message or the paper Export Accompanying Document (EAD) endorsed by the German customs office
  • Transport evidence: original Bill of Lading (Konnossement) for sea freight, CMR waybill with customs admission stamp (Ausfuhrvermerk) for road freight, or Air Waybill for air freight
  • Commercial invoice (original) with UDI/LOT numbers, Incoterms, net and gross weights, and the export statement 'Lieferung ins Drittland (Ausfuhr)'
  • Export proof (Ausfuhrnachweis) compiled under Secs. 8-13 UStDV combining the customs declaration with transport documents, retained for 10 years
  • Banking evidence: SWIFT MT103 or bank account statement reflecting receipt of export proceeds; Germany imposes no Inward Remittance Certificate requirement

Since 1 January 2025, all German B2B recipients must be able to receive structured e-invoices (E-Rechnung) for domestic supplies in XRechnung (UBL 2.1/Peppol BIS) or ZUGFeRD 2.0/Factur-X formats. Issuance of E-Rechnung is mandatory from 1 January 2027 for businesses with turnover above EUR 800,000 and for all businesses from 1 January 2028. Germany currently operates no real-time fiscal-clearance API, no government digital stamping, and no mandatory national e-invoicing network; the Peppol network is permitted but not compulsory, and invoice data under the GoBD must be archived for 10 years. Cross-border exports are cleared electronically through the German customs ATLAS system with automatic IE-529 confirmation messages. The EU ViDA package will introduce continuous transaction controls and intra-Community e-invoice reporting obligations in stages expected around 2028-2030.

The Finanzamt applies rigorous substantive scrutiny to zero-rated export claims. Failure to substantiate the export timely triggers retrospective VAT assessment at 19% plus interest of 0.5% per month (Sec. 233a AO) from 15 months after the close of the assessment year, and a late-payment surcharge of 1% per month on the overdue VAT (Sec. 240 AO, maximum 10 years). Intentional misdeclaration constitutes criminal tax evasion under Sec. 370 AO with imprisonment of up to 5 years (6 months to 10 years for particularly severe cases) and daily-rate criminal fines; negligent understatement attracts administrative fines of up to EUR 50,000 under Sec. 378 AO. Violations of statutory record-keeping duties under Secs. 143-147 AO and fraudulent export-credit claims additionally incur fines of up to EUR 5,000 under Sec. 26a UStG, exclusion from future zero-rating, retrospective clawback of recovered input VAT, and reclassification of the transactions as taxable domestic supplies.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Germany operates under the Eurozone capital and current account convertibility regime administered by the Deutsche Bundesbank. Capital account transactions are generally permitted with prior notification for amounts exceeding EUR 12,500 per transaction. Current account transactions, including profit remittances to foreign parent entities, are unrestricted and freely convertible. Profit remittance is subject only to standard corporate tax compliance, beneficial ownership reporting, and AML verification; no material convertibility or remittance hurdles exist for qualifying German UG entities.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Exporters must surrender 100% of foreign exchange proceeds from exports to third countries within 30 calendar days of receipt. Mandatory conversion occurs at the official ECB reference rate published by the Deutsche Bundesbank. Intra-EU trade proceeds are exempt from surrender requirements.

Central bank supervision: Deutsche Bundesbank

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Revolut Fit: High

Digital EMIs through licensed providers such as N26 Business or Revolut Business are feasible for cross-border operations, subject to full BaFin licensing, AML/KYC compliance, and German payment services regulations. Offshore digital wallets remain restricted due to local oversight requirements.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% German corporate income tax under the Corporate Income Tax Act, plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on the corporate income tax, producing an aggregate federal burden of 15.825%. Trade tax is imposed separately by the municipality; the combined corporate and trade tax burden commonly ranges from approximately 23% to 33%, depending on the municipal multiplier.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% capital income withholding tax plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on the withholding tax, producing an effective domestic rate of 26.375%, before any church tax. Under the EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive as implemented in Germany, qualifying corporate recipients may obtain full exemption where the minimum participation, holding-period, subject-to-tax, anti-abuse and beneficial-ownership requirements are satisfied. German domestic relief may also be available for qualifying corporate shareholders under the participation exemption and withholding-tax procedures.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

For a calendar-year taxpayer, the statutory annual filing deadline is generally seven months after the end of the tax year, normally 31 July of the following year. Where a tax adviser prepares the return, the extended deadline is generally the last day of February of the second following year, subject to statutory exceptions and administrative extension rules. A return may be required earlier by individual order. If the fiscal year differs from the calendar year, the deadline is calculated from the end of that fiscal year.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: German taxpayers must maintain records for cross-border related-party transactions under Section 90 of the Fiscal Code and the German Transfer Pricing Documentation Ordinance. For extraordinary transactions, quantitative thresholds commonly relevant to detailed documentation are EUR 5 million for transactions involving tangible or intangible assets and EUR 500,000 for transactions involving services. A master file is generally required where the German entity's sales revenue exceeds EUR 100 million in the relevant financial year and the group is part of a multinational enterprise. Documentation must be available within the statutory response period after a tax authority request; Germany generally does not operate a universal annual local-file filing requirement, but non-compliance can result in estimation and documentation surcharges.

Economic substance scope: Germany has no standalone regime titled Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to certain low-tax jurisdictions. Substance is nevertheless relevant under German transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-abuse rule, treaty beneficial-ownership standards, anti-treaty-shopping provisions, controlled foreign company rules, anti-hybrid rules and permanent-establishment analysis. The UG should maintain real decision-making, appropriately qualified personnel, operational premises, commercial risks, financial capacity, accounting records, board and management evidence, customer and supplier contracts and contemporaneous business rationale. A mailbox entity, conduit payment chain, circular financing, nominal director arrangement or undocumented management-fee structure may result in denial of deductions, treaty benefits, withholding-tax relief or corporate separateness protections.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Filings

Statutory sanction: Tax evasion under Section 370 of the Fiscal Code may result in a fine or imprisonment of up to five years; particularly serious cases may result in imprisonment from six months to ten years. The unpaid tax, interest and administrative penalties remain recoverable. Voluntary disclosure relief is strictly limited and unavailable in many cases after discovery or investigation has commenced.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may incur personal tax liability under Sections 34 and 69 of the Fiscal Code for culpable failure to discharge the company's tax obligations. Directors and responsible employees may face criminal prosecution, asset seizure, professional disqualification and imprisonment. Civil restitution may include unpaid tax, interest, penalties and losses caused to the company or creditors. Travel restrictions are not automatic but may be ordered in criminal or enforcement proceedings where legally justified.
Withholding-Tax Failure

Statutory sanction: The German payer may be assessed for the tax not withheld or remitted, together with interest and penalties. Intentional or reckless violations may constitute tax evasion or tax understatement and can generate criminal liability.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may be personally liable where they culpably failed to ensure withholding and remittance. Foreign recipient status does not transfer the payer's statutory responsibility. Criminal exposure may include fines or imprisonment where the conduct is intentional.
Transfer Pricing Misstatement

Statutory sanction: The tax authority may adjust taxable income, deny deductions, impose late-payment interest and apply documentation-related surcharges. Where records are missing or unusable, the authority may estimate the taxable result and impose a surcharge commonly ranging from 5% to 10% of the adjustment, subject to statutory minimums, caps and exceptions.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal tax liability for culpable non-compliance and civil claims for losses caused by negligent governance. Intentional concealment may create tax-criminal exposure. A foreign group policy or parent-company instruction does not eliminate German director duties.
Shell Company and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: Treaty benefits and domestic withholding exemptions may be denied under anti-treaty-shopping and general anti-abuse rules. Tax may be reassessed at the domestic rate, with interest, penalties and potential criminal consequences if facts were intentionally misrepresented.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for unpaid withholding tax and may face civil claims, disqualification consequences and criminal prosecution for fraudulent or intentionally abusive conduct. Imprisonment is fact-dependent and generally requires proof of intentional criminal conduct.
Permanent Establishment Concealment

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include retrospective corporate income tax, trade tax, VAT and payroll assessments, interest, late-filing penalties, bookkeeping penalties and tax-evasion liability where the omission was intentional.

Director personal liability: Responsible managers may incur personal liability for unpaid taxes and social-security amounts where statutory duties were breached. Criminal sanctions may include fines or imprisonment in serious intentional cases.
Accounting and Record-Keeping Failure

Statutory sanction: The tax authority may reject records, estimate the tax base, impose accounting and documentation penalties, deny deductions and assess additional tax and interest. Commercial-law breaches may produce separate fines and director liability.

Director personal liability: Managing directors are responsible for proper organization, books, filings and document retention. Culpable failure may result in personal tax liability, civil claims, insolvency liability and criminal sanctions for intentional bookkeeping or filing misconduct.
VAT Fraud and Carousel Transactions

Statutory sanction: VAT may be denied or reassessed, with repayment demands, interest, fines and tax-evasion prosecution. Serious VAT fraud may result in imprisonment under the Fiscal Code and general criminal law.

Director personal liability: Directors and responsible officers may be personally liable for unpaid VAT and may face asset seizure, civil restitution, director disqualification and imprisonment where intentional participation or grossly culpable oversight is established.
AML, Beneficial Ownership and Sanctions

Statutory sanction: Violations may trigger administrative fines under the German Money Laundering Act, transaction blocking, account closure, confiscation and criminal sanctions under German sanctions and criminal law. Sanctions violations may carry substantial fines and imprisonment depending on the conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally fined or prosecuted for intentional or negligent compliance failures. Civil exposure includes restitution and losses caused to the company, creditors or counterparties. Travel restrictions and asset freezes may arise in criminal or sanctions proceedings but are not automatic.
Social Security and Payroll Non-Remittance

Statutory sanction: Unpaid payroll taxes and social-security contributions may be assessed retroactively with interest, surcharges and fines. Withholding employee social-security contributions and failing to remit them may constitute a criminal offense under Section 266a of the Criminal Code.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may be personally liable for unpaid social-security contributions and payroll taxes and may face criminal prosecution, fines or imprisonment. Insolvency proceedings can materially increase personal exposure.
Insolvency Delay and Creditor Prejudice

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include repayment of prohibited payments, insolvency administrator claims, civil damages, fines and criminal sanctions under insolvency and criminal law. Tax debts and social-security debts may remain enforceable notwithstanding insolvency.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may be personally liable for prohibited payments, tax and social-security losses, creditor prejudice and delayed filing. Criminal penalties may include fines or imprisonment; travel restrictions may be imposed in criminal or insolvency proceedings where statutory conditions are met.
Illegal Profit Distribution and Capital Maintenance Breach

Statutory sanction: The distribution may be void or repayable, and recipients may be required to return funds. Additional tax, withholding tax, interest, insolvency and commercial-law consequences may arise.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable to the company for repayment and damages and may face insolvency-related claims. Intentional asset diversion, fraudulent accounting or creditor prejudice may result in criminal prosecution and imprisonment.
Medical-Device Regulatory Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Regulatory measures may include prohibition of sale, recall, seizure, administrative fines, market withdrawal and criminal sanctions under the EU Medical Device Regulation as implemented by German law. Tax treatment does not cure product-regulatory non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil product-liability claims, regulatory fines, criminal prosecution for intentional or negligent violations and personal liability for losses caused by compliance failures. Imprisonment is fact-dependent and generally requires serious intentional or negligent conduct.
