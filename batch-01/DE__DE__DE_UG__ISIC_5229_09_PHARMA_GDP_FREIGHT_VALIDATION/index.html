Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Germany operates within the European Economic and Monetary Union; the euro is fully and freely convertible with no exchange-control restrictions. Capital transactions and current-account payments are liberalized pursuant to Articles 63–65 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union and the German Foreign Trade and Payments Act (Außenwirtschaftsgesetz – AWG). Profit remittance, dividend distribution, and capital repatriation by a non-resident shareholder of a UG require no central-bank approval and are not subject to quota, queue, or conversion-ratio limitations. The only residual obligation is statistical: residents and resident-account holders must report cross-border payments received or made in excess of €12,500 per counterparty to the Bundesbank under the AWV for balance-of-payments compilation, with no impact on convertibility.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory timeline for surrender of export proceeds and no mandatory conversion ratio into euro. Export receipts from GDP pharma freight services may be retained indefinitely in any freely usable currency, held in foreign-denominated accounts with German or EEA banks, or converted at commercial discretion. The sole binding duty is the AWV statistical reporting threshold of €12,500 per counterparty, which must be declared by the recipient via the Bundesbank's electronic reporting channels by the fifth banking day of the month following receipt; non-compliance attracts administrative fines.

Central bank supervision: Deutsche Bundesbank (Frankfurt am Main) — the independent central bank of the Federal Republic of Germany, acting as the competent authority for foreign exchange surveillance, external trade statistics, and AWV cross-border payment reporting; the European Central Bank (ECB) exercises the common euro-area monetary authority and lender-of-last-resort functions.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility Rating: Medium as a supplementary rail; Low as the sole treasury infrastructure. An offshore or EEA fintech operating as an electronic money institution (EMI) under PSD2 may lawfully issue e-money and execute SEPA payments, but it cannot substitute a German-regulated demand-deposit account for statutory capital administration, tax debiting, customs duty payments, or the Bundesbank AWV reporting workflow. Because the UG's business scope comprises GDP-certified clinical logistics with tight chain-of-custody traceability, at least one German-resident bank (or the German branch of an EEA bank holding a KWG banking licence) must be the primary account holder; neobanks such as Penta, N26 Business, or Kontist may be accepted as secondary transaction accounts provided the primary clearing account remains with a BaFin-regulated institution.

Share-capital subscriptions into the UG must be routed either by SWIFT MT103 (foreign currency) or by SEPA Credit Transfer (euro) to the UG's named blocked capital-deposit account at Commerzbank AG or the primary house bank, using the remittance fields: exact legal name 'Unternehmergesellschaft (haftungsbeschränkung)', the notarial file reference, and the designation 'Kapitalkonto – Einlage' or 'Sacheinlage'. Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% federal corporate income tax (Körperschaftsteuer), plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on that tax, producing a federal corporate-tax burden of 15.825%. A UG is also generally subject to municipal trade tax (Gewerbesteuer): the federal base rate is 3.5%, multiplied by the municipality’s assessment factor. The combined corporate and trade tax burden therefore varies by municipality and is commonly around 30%; no single national combined rate applies.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% withholding tax plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on the withholding tax, producing an aggregate domestic rate of 26.375%, before any church tax or relief. A lower rate or exemption may be available under an applicable tax treaty or the EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive, subject to ownership, holding, beneficial-ownership, anti-abuse, and procedural requirements.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Generally, annual returns are due by 31 July of the following calendar year. Where a tax adviser or other authorized professional prepares the return, the ordinary statutory deadline is generally the last day of February of the second following calendar year, subject to statutory extensions and special-year rules. An individual notice may set an earlier deadline. The relevant assessment-year rules must be checked for the particular filing year.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: For cross-border related-party transactions, German documentation thresholds generally require a Local File for annual transaction categories exceeding EUR 6 million for supplies of goods or EUR 600,000 for other transactions, including services. A Master File is generally required where the German entity belongs to a multinational group whose consolidated revenue in the preceding financial year is at least EUR 100 million. Documentation, transaction-category definitions, contemporaneous preparation, submission periods, and exceptions are governed by the Fiscal Code and implementing rules; documentation may be requested by the tax authority.

Economic substance scope: Germany does not have a general separate regime formally titled Economic Substance Regulations (ESR) comparable to certain offshore jurisdictions. Nevertheless, German tax and treaty outcomes depend on actual facts, business functions, personnel, decision-making, assets, and risk assumption. Anti-abuse provisions, including section 42 of the Fiscal Code and section 50d(3) of the Income Tax Act, may deny tax benefits in artificial or insufficiently substantiated structures. Related-party pricing must reflect the arm’s-length principle; controlled foreign company, interest-limitation, anti-hybrid, and beneficial-ownership rules may also apply.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and false tax reporting

Statutory sanction: Section 370 of the Fiscal Code provides for imprisonment of up to five years or a fine; particularly serious cases carry imprisonment from six months to ten years. Back taxes, interest, and tax-related surcharges may also be assessed. Negligent tax reduction is separately sanctionable under section 378.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may face personal criminal liability where they act, direct, or knowingly facilitate the offence. They may also incur personal liability for unpaid taxes under section 69 of the Fiscal Code where a culpable breach of tax duties causes non-collection. Imprisonment is possible following conviction. A general travel ban or automatic piercing of the corporate veil does not follow solely from a tax assessment; procedural restrictions require a specific legal basis.
Late filing, non-payment, or failure to remit withholding tax

Statutory sanction: Late-filing surcharges, late-payment surcharges, interest, estimated assessments, and enforcement action may apply. A culpable failure to withhold or remit tax may create additional liability and, depending on the facts, administrative or criminal exposure.

Director personal liability: A managing director may be personally liable under section 69 of the Fiscal Code for culpable failure to discharge the company’s tax duties, including withholding and remittance obligations. Liability is fact-specific and is not automatic merely because the company is insolvent.
Transfer-pricing non-compliance and undocumented related-party transactions

Statutory sanction: Tax adjustments, additional tax and interest, and statutory documentation-related surcharges may apply. In specified cases of missing or unusable documentation, the tax authority may estimate income and apply statutory surcharges under the Fiscal Code.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal tax or criminal exposure if they knowingly participate in false reporting or culpably breach tax duties. Ordinary transfer-pricing adjustments do not by themselves pierce the corporate veil.
Abusive treaty or directive claims and artificial conduit structures

Statutory sanction: Relief may be denied, withholding tax and interest may be assessed, and penalties may apply if declarations were false or duties were breached. Section 50d(3) of the Income Tax Act and general anti-abuse rules may be relevant.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal liability or criminal exposure where they knowingly submit false claims or culpably fail to discharge withholding duties. Denial of treaty relief alone does not automatically create personal liability.
Corporate insolvency and creditor prejudice

Statutory sanction: The Insolvency Code imposes filing duties and potential restitution or liability for prohibited payments; criminal consequences may arise under applicable insolvency offences. A UG’s limited liability does not protect directors from their own statutory breaches.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may be personally liable for prohibited post-insolvency payments and other statutory breaches and may face criminal penalties where the offence requirements are met. Travel restrictions are not an automatic consequence and require a specific legal basis.
AML, sanctions, or falsified trade documentation

Statutory sanction: Depending on the conduct, sanctions may include administrative fines, confiscation, trade or customs consequences, and criminal penalties under applicable German and EU law. The precise sanction depends on the offence and facts.

Director personal liability: Directors and responsible employees may be personally prosecuted for their own participation or culpable failures where a statutory duty applies. Corporate penalties and confiscation may also be imposed where legally available; liability is not automatic solely by reason of office.
GDP, pharmaceutical logistics, and regulated-activity non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Regulatory measures may include corrective orders, suspension or withdrawal of authorization, administrative fines, and, for conduct meeting statutory offence elements, criminal penalties. The exact requirements depend on the activities performed and the competent authority.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal administrative or criminal liability for their own conduct or culpable breaches of applicable duties. Tax consequences may arise separately; GDP non-compliance does not itself establish corporate income tax liability or automatically pierce the corporate veil.
