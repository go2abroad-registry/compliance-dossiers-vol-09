Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

The competent authority for tax registration is the locally responsible Finanzamt. The Federal Central Tax Office (Bundeszentralamt für Steuern, BZSt) issues German VAT identification numbers (Umsatzsteuer-Identifikationsnummer, USt-IdNr.) through its online VAT ID application service. Tax registration and VAT questionnaires are generally submitted electronically through the ELSTER portal.

E-government filing portal

A UG must be registered with the competent Finanzamt after incorporation and commencement of business. The company submits the electronic questionnaire for tax registration (Fragebogen zur steuerlichen Erfassung) through ELSTER, providing its commercial-register details, registered office, business activities, bank details, expected turnover, expected VAT liabilities, and requested tax regimes. The Finanzamt issues the tax number (Steuernummer). A separate USt-IdNr. is requested from the BZSt through the BZSt online service or through the tax registration process. A foreign shareholder or foreign director may be required to provide identification, certified corporate documents, proof of registered address, powers of attorney, and evidence of the German business activity. The tax number and VAT ID are separate identifiers; the VAT ID is used principally for EU cross-border transactions.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — A UG must be registered with the competent Finanzamt after incorporation and commencement of business. The company submits the electronic questionnaire for tax registration (Fragebogen zur steuerlichen Erfassung) through ELSTER, providing its commercial-register details, registered office, business activities, bank details, expected turnover, expected VAT liabilities, and requested tax regimes. The tax number and VAT ID are separate identifiers; the VAT ID is used principally for EU cross-border transactions.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A UG is generally treated as a corporation for German corporate income tax and trade tax purposes; Germany has no US-style check-the-box election for a UG. The entity is subject to Körperschaftsteuer, solidarity surcharge, and municipal Gewerbesteuer. A UG may elect or apply for statutory VAT treatments where conditions are met, including the small-business exemption under Section 19 of the German VAT Act (UStG), although this is generally unsuitable for a machinery wholesaler seeking input VAT recovery. Optional VAT taxation of certain exempt supplies and other statutory VAT elections may apply. German implementation of the Anti-Tax Avoidance Directive and hybrid-mismatch rules can deny deductions or impose limitations; those rules do not provide a general entity-classification election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

19%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

There is no general turnover threshold exempting an ordinary German business from VAT registration and compliance. The small-business regime under Section 19 UStG may apply where German turnover did not exceed EUR 25,000 in the preceding calendar year and does not exceed EUR 100,000 in the current calendar year, subject to statutory conditions and annual assessment. A business using the regime does not charge VAT and generally cannot deduct input VAT. A German UG trading used excavators will normally register for VAT regardless of the small-business thresholds.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods from Germany to destinations outside the European Union may qualify for VAT exemption with input VAT recovery under the German VAT Act where the goods are physically transported or dispatched to a non-EU destination and the supplier retains prescribed documentary and accounting evidence of export. The supplier must establish the foreign destination, the identity of the purchaser where required, the transport or dispatch facts, and the relationship between the sale and the export. For used excavators, serial numbers, photographs, transport records, customs exit confirmation, and delivery documentation should be retained. Intra-EU supplies to a VAT-registered customer may qualify for VAT exemption as an intra-Community supply, subject to valid customer VAT identification, transport evidence, and recapitulative-statement compliance. Services are not generally 'zero-rated'; their VAT treatment depends on place-of-supply rules. B2B services are generally taxed at the recipient's location, subject to exceptions, while certain services connected with goods or immovable property have special rules.

Germany does not currently operate a general real-time invoice-clearance system or universal digital tax-stamping requirement. From 1 January 2025, German businesses must be able to receive structured electronic invoices for domestic business-to-business transactions. Mandatory issuance of structured e-invoices is phased in: generally from 1 January 2027 for businesses with prior-year domestic turnover above EUR 800,000 and from 1 January 2028 for other affected transactions, subject to statutory transitional rules. Permitted structured formats include XRechnung and compliant ZUGFeRD invoices. PDF invoices remain usable during applicable transition periods and for transactions outside the structured e-invoice mandate. Public-sector invoicing may require XRechnung or other prescribed formats. Electronic records must comply with German retention and accounting requirements, including the GoBD.

Germany has no general statutory requirement to repatriate export proceeds within 180 days. Export proceeds may remain abroad, subject to ordinary accounting, customs, anti-money-laundering, transfer-pricing, and foreign-exchange reporting requirements. If export exemption or input VAT recovery is unsupported, the Finanzamt may retrospectively assess German VAT, deny or claw back input VAT credits, impose late-payment interest under the German Fiscal Code, and correct prior VAT returns. Deliberate or reckless false documentation may result in administrative fines, tax-evasion prosecution, corporate and individual liability, customs penalties, loss of VAT compliance credibility, and additional interest. Criminal tax evasion can carry imprisonment or fines for responsible individuals; particularly serious cases may attract materially higher sanctions. Customs violations may separately trigger customs debt, import or export restrictions, fines, or criminal liability.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Germany maintains full convertibility for both capital and current account transactions under EU primary law and the ECB's single-market framework. The euro is freely convertible with no prior exchange-control approval for profit remittance, shareholder loans, dividends, or trade-related payments. Practical hurdles arise from Germany's withholding tax regime on dividends (25% plus solidarity surcharge and, where applicable, church tax, subject to tax treaty relief), the obligation to file tax returns before using debtor accounts for pass-through flows, and enhanced bank documentary requirements for funds originating from non-EU or high-risk jurisdictions. No distinction is made between current and capital account restrictions in ordinary commercial banking; the only material restrictions are EU sanctions, export-control rules for dual-use or strategic goods, and the AWV statistical reporting duty for cross-border payments in excess of defined thresholds.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Germany and the euro area impose no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement and no mandatory conversion ratio. Exporters may retain sale proceeds in EUR or any foreign currency, may credit them to foreign accounts, and may freely repatriate capital. The only compliance duty is statistical: a German resident engaging in cross-border payments or receipts of €12,500 or more per transaction, or aggregated monthly counterpart flows exceeding this threshold, must report to the Deutsche Bundesbank in accordance with AWV §67 for balance-of-payments statistics. Export invoices and shipping documents must be retained to support the declared purpose codes and to satisfy any Bundesbank query during data-quality checks.

Central bank supervision: Deutsche Bundesbank (national central bank of Germany; exercises monetary authority jointly with the European Central Bank within the Eurosystem; responsible for external sector statistics and cross-border payment reporting under the German Foreign Trade and Payments Regulation (Außenwirtschaftsverordnung, AWV)).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for operational clearing, Low for capital escrow and documentary trade instruments. EEA-authorised Electronic Money Institutions may passport into Germany and provide e-money accounts, IBANs, FX conversion, and intra-EEA SEPA payments without a German banking license. However, such EMIs cannot legally issue physical letters of credit, cannot accept deposits in the strict German supervisory sense, and generally lack established correspondent-heavy documentary trade rails. Non-EEA offshore digital EMIs are not permitted to conduct payment services in Germany unless they hold a properly passported authorisation or a BaFin license; engaging with them for core treasury operations is legally and operationally imprudent. Use a fintech EMI only as a secondary collection or disbursement rail for small-value operating expenses.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound shareholder equity or subordinated loan capital must be routed through the standard SWIFT correspondent-banking system using an outgoing MT103 from the non-resident shareholder's bank to the UG's account at its German bank. The payment must carry an accurate purpose code and a clear narrative in field 70: e.g., 'EQUITY CONTRIBUTION TO UG CASH CAPITAL INCREASE' or 'SHAREHOLDER LOAN TO UG (NOTA DIPENDENT)'. German bank onboarding will require the notarised capital increase resolution, the commercial register filing or pending-filing certificate, confirmation by the notary that the contribution was paid in cash and placed at the managing director's free disposal, and evidence that the UG's capital reserves comply with the UG's retained-earnings rule. For cross-border payments in excess of the AWV reporting threshold, the UG must file the Bundesbank external-sector report for balance-of-payments statistics. For payments below €12,500 per month or transaction, no prior report is required, but the bank may still require documentation for its own internal reporting under German anti-money-laundering law.Trade settlements for the purchase of used excavators and rollers from non-EU suppliers use three main clearing rails: (1) SWIFT MT103 direct wire transfers for supplier payments, supported by MT202 cover wires for interbank reimbursements; (2) commercial documentary credits (Letters of Credit) issued by a German bank under UCP600, transmitted via SWIFT MT700 series, with advising or confirming branches in the supplier's jurisdiction; and (3) documentary collections (DP/DA) using SWIFT MT400 series or proprietary bank networks for lower-risk trades. For EUR-denominated payments within the EU or to participant banks, payments settle in central bank money via TARGET2 or via SEPA CT for smaller amounts. All outbound disbursements should reference the underlying commercial invoice, machine serial numbers, Incoterms rule, and transport documents to satisfy anti-money-laundering and sanctions screening. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% German corporate income tax (Körperschaftsteuer), plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on the corporate income tax, equivalent to 0.825% of taxable income. Trade tax (Gewerbesteuer) is imposed separately by the municipality; the combined effective burden for a typical German trading company is commonly approximately 22.8% to 33%, with approximately 29.8% at a 400% municipal trade-tax multiplier.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% German withholding tax on dividends, plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on the withholding tax, producing an aggregate statutory withholding of 26.375%. The domestic rate may be reduced or eliminated under an applicable tax treaty, the EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive as implemented in Germany, or a statutory exemption. The German participation exemption for qualifying corporate shareholders generally requires a direct participation of at least 10% for corporate income tax purposes; additional anti-abuse and holding-period conditions may apply for withholding-tax relief.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

For a calendar-year taxpayer, annual corporate income tax and trade tax returns are generally due by 31 July of the following year. For a non-calendar fiscal year, the deadline is calculated by reference to the end of the fiscal year. VAT advance returns are generally due by the tenth day after the end of the reporting period, with possible permanent extension arrangements.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: For cross-border related-party transactions, a Local File is generally required when the annual value of cross-border supplies of goods exceeds EUR 6 million or the annual value of other cross-border transactions, including services, financing or royalties, exceeds EUR 600,000. A Master File is generally required for German entities belonging to an internationally active group where the group's consolidated revenue exceeds EUR 100 million and the statutory conditions are met. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to multinational groups with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million. Documentation must be prepared within the statutory deadlines and supplied to the tax authority upon request; extraordinary business transactions may require prompt documentation.

Economic substance scope: Germany does not operate a single standalone OECD-style Economic Substance Regulation covering all entities. Substance is assessed through the arm's-length principle, the general anti-abuse rule in Section 42 of the Fiscal Code, beneficial-ownership rules, treaty anti-abuse provisions, the German anti-treaty-shopping rule in Section 50d(3) of the Income Tax Act, controlled foreign company rules, interest limitation rules and EU anti-hybrid rules. The UG should maintain German commercial premises or operational capacity appropriate to its functions, personnel or outsourced operational control, inventory and logistics evidence, decision-making records, bank-account control, customer and supplier contracts, and commercial risk documentation. A conduit company with no meaningful functions, risks or assets may lose treaty or withholding-tax benefits and may face recharacterization of transactions.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and False Tax Reporting

Statutory sanction: Under Section 370 of the German Fiscal Code, intentional tax evasion is punishable by a fine or imprisonment of up to five years. Particularly serious cases may result in imprisonment from six months to ten years. Tax, interest, late-payment surcharges and enforcement costs remain payable. Attempted evasion may also be punishable.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may be criminally liable as responsible decision-makers and may be personally liable for unpaid taxes under Section 69 of the Fiscal Code where culpable breach of tax duties is established. Civil restitution includes the unpaid tax and related charges. A travel ban is not an automatic tax penalty, but criminal proceedings may produce judicial or immigration-related restrictions, asset freezes or passport measures in appropriate cases.
VAT Carousel Fraud and False Intra-Community Trade

Statutory sanction: The conduct may constitute tax evasion, aiding tax evasion, document offences and organized criminal conduct. Sanctions can include imprisonment up to five years, or six months to ten years in particularly serious cases, repayment of VAT, denial of input VAT and loss of zero-rating.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for VAT and may face criminal liability if they knew or recklessly facilitated the fraud. Authorities may impose VAT assessments, secure assets, restrict bank operations and pursue confiscation of criminal proceeds. Travel restrictions are case-specific and not automatic.
Withholding-Tax Evasion and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: The UG may be assessed for the unwithheld tax, solidarity surcharge, interest and late-payment charges. Intentional non-withholding or false applications may constitute tax evasion or tax-related offences. Treaty and EU relief may be denied under anti-abuse rules.

Director personal liability: The managing director may be personally liable for withholding tax under Section 69 of the Fiscal Code where the failure resulted from culpable breach of duty. The recipient may also face tax assessment in its residence or source jurisdiction. Criminal sanctions depend on intent and the amount involved.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation and Hidden Profit Distribution

Statutory sanction: The tax authority may make an adjustment, reclassify the amount as a hidden profit distribution, deny deductions, impose additional corporate income tax and trade tax, and assess withholding tax. Documentation failures may trigger a statutory surcharge generally ranging from EUR 5,000 to EUR 1 million, subject to statutory calculation rules and minimum adjustments.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur civil liability to the UG for unlawful distributions or negligent tax damage and may be liable for resulting withholding tax. Criminal liability requires intentional or otherwise criminal conduct, such as deliberate tax evasion or falsification.
Customs, Import VAT and Origin Fraud

Statutory sanction: The authorities may recover customs duty and import VAT, impose customs penalties and interest, seize goods and prosecute customs or tax offences. Serious intentional conduct may constitute tax evasion, smuggling or document fraud.

Director personal liability: The responsible director or customs representative may be personally or criminally liable where the breach was intentional or culpably caused. Goods, sale proceeds and related assets may be confiscated in criminal proceedings.
Anti-Money-Laundering and Beneficial-Ownership Failures

Statutory sanction: Money laundering under Section 261 of the Criminal Code may be punishable by a fine or imprisonment of up to five years; aggravated cases may carry imprisonment from six months to ten years. Breaches of the German Money Laundering Act may result in administrative fines, generally up to EUR 100,000 or higher statutory amounts tied to the economic benefit or turnover depending on the violation and regulated-person status.

Director personal liability: Directors and compliance-responsible persons may face individual administrative or criminal liability for participation, concealment or intentional facilitation. Failure to maintain beneficial-owner filings may result in administrative fines and public-register consequences. Travel restrictions are not automatic but may follow criminal investigation or judicial orders.
Sanctions and Export-Control Breaches

Statutory sanction: Violations of EU sanctions implemented under German law may result in substantial administrative fines, criminal prosecution, confiscation and imprisonment, with the exact sanction depending on the measure, intent, value and applicable statute.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally prosecuted for intentional or negligent breaches where the relevant statute permits. The UG may be fined under corporate-offence rules and may lose access to banking, customs and public-contracting relationships.
Corporate Insolvency and Wrongful Trading

Statutory sanction: Under the German Insolvency Code, a managing director must generally file without undue delay and no later than three weeks after insolvency due to inability to pay, or six weeks after over-indebtedness, provided that the statutory rescue conditions are satisfied. Breach may be criminally punishable by imprisonment of up to three years or a fine, with additional liability for prohibited payments and insolvency-related offences.

Director personal liability: Directors may owe civil restitution to the insolvency estate, be liable for prohibited payments and face disqualification consequences. Criminal courts may impose travel or reporting restrictions where appropriate, but no automatic travel ban applies.
Accounting, Record-Keeping and Annual-Account Failures

Statutory sanction: The UG may face late-filing and administrative fines, estimated tax assessments, loss of tax deductions, enforcement and, where accounts are intentionally falsified, criminal liability for accounting or insolvency-related offences.

Director personal liability: Managing directors are responsible for proper accounting, tax records and annual-account filing. They may face civil damages, personal tax liability and criminal prosecution where intentional falsification or culpable non-compliance is established.
Illegal Distributions and Capital-Maintenance Breaches

Statutory sanction: Unlawful distributions may be recoverable by the UG and may constitute breaches of the German Limited Liability Companies Act. The UG must generally allocate one-quarter of annual surplus reduced by loss carryforwards to the statutory reserve until the required capital level is reached.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable to the UG for prohibited payments and to creditors in insolvency-related circumstances. Shareholders may be required to repay unlawful distributions. Criminal liability depends on the conduct, intent and applicable insolvency or fraud provisions.
Bribery, Commercial Corruption and Fictitious Intermediaries

Statutory sanction: German bribery and commercial-corruption offences may result in fines, imprisonment, confiscation of proceeds and corporate fines. Tax deductions for bribes are denied, and related payments may trigger additional tax and withholding assessments.

Director personal liability: Directors and employees may be personally prosecuted. The company may face confiscation, corporate fines, debarment and reputational or banking consequences. Travel restrictions are case-specific and depend on the criminal proceedings.
