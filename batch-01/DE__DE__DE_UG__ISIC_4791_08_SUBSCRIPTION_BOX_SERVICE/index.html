The Federal Central Tax Office (Bundeszentralamt für Steuern, BZSt) issues the German VAT identification number (Umsatzsteuer-Identifikationsnummer, USt-IdNr.). Tax registration is initiated through the ELSTER electronic tax platform (Elektronische Steuererklärung) and the electronic questionnaire for tax registration (Fragebogen zur steuerlichen Erfassung).

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A UG is a corporation subject to German corporate income tax and trade tax and has no general United States-style check-the-box election. The corporate tax treatment follows the legal form. The partnership corporate-tax option under Section 1a of the German Corporate Income Tax Act is generally unavailable to a UG. A UG may participate in a statutory tax group (Organschaft) if the ownership, financial integration, profit-transfer agreement, and other statutory conditions are satisfied. The VAT small-business exemption under Section 19 of the German VAT Act is an exemption election or status, not an entity classification election. German anti-hybrid mismatch rules implement the EU Anti-Tax Avoidance Directive and do not provide an elective hybrid classification regime.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

19%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

There is no general turnover threshold below which a domestic business may disregard German VAT registration when making taxable supplies. The small-business exemption under Section 19 UStG may apply where prior-calendar-year domestic turnover did not exceed EUR 25,000 and current-calendar-year turnover does not exceed EUR 100,000, subject to statutory conditions and the applicable effective-date rules. The exemption prevents charging VAT but generally eliminates input-tax recovery. For EU cross-border distance sales and certain electronically supplied services, the EU-wide EUR 10,000 threshold may apply, with VAT due in the customer Member State above the threshold or where the One-Stop Shop is elected.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods exported from Germany to a destination outside the European Union may qualify for VAT exemption with input-tax recovery under the German export-supply rules where the goods leave the EU customs territory, the supplier or customer transports or dispatches them as required, and the supplier retains prescribed documentary or accounting evidence of export. Intra-Community supplies of goods may qualify for exemption where the goods are transported to another EU Member State, the customer is a taxable person or qualifying legal entity, the customer provides a valid VAT identification number, the reporting requirements are satisfied, and the supplier maintains transport evidence. Services are not automatically zero-rated as exports. Their VAT treatment generally follows the place-of-supply rules, including the business-customer rule and special rules for electronically supplied, real-estate-related, transport, event, and consumer services. A subscription box involving goods normally requires separate analysis for domestic supplies, intra-Community distance sales, exports, import VAT, and marketplace or fulfillment arrangements.

  • Export customs declaration or equivalent electronic export record, including the Movement Reference Number where applicable
  • Electronic confirmation of exit from the EU customs territory or other legally accepted export evidence
  • Commercial invoice, order record, packing list, and transport documentation
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, consignment note, courier record, or equivalent transport evidence where relevant
  • Proof identifying the supplier, goods, destination outside the EU, and linkage between the export transaction and the customs record
  • For exempt intra-Community supplies: customer VAT identification number, transport evidence, and correctly filed EC Sales List (Zusammenfassende Meldung)
  • Inward remittance evidence may support the audit file but is not generally a mandatory German condition for export VAT exemption, and Germany has no general requirement for an inward-remittance certificate

Germany does not operate a general real-time VAT invoice-clearance or digital-stamping system. From 1 January 2025, domestic businesses must be able to receive structured electronic invoices compliant with the German and European standards, including XRechnung and permissible ZUGFeRD formats. Mandatory issuance of structured B2B domestic e-invoices is subject to transitional rules: generally from 1 January 2027 for businesses with prior-year turnover exceeding EUR 800,000 and from 1 January 2028 for all affected businesses, subject to statutory exceptions and further legislative implementation. During the transition, paper invoices and other electronic invoices may remain permissible in specified circumstances, commonly with recipient consent. Electronic VAT records, invoice integrity, retention, and audit-access requirements remain applicable. The European Union VAT in the Digital Age reforms may modify the future reporting and e-invoicing framework.

Germany has no general statutory 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for qualifying export supplies. Tax authorities may deny or claw back the exemption and assess output VAT retroactively if export, transport, customer-status, reporting, or documentary requirements are not proven. Additional amounts may include late-payment interest under the German Fiscal Code, correction of VAT returns, suspension or denial of input-tax refunds, and liability for incorrectly stated or fraudulently claimed VAT. Intentional or reckless conduct may constitute a tax offence or tax misdemeanor, including tax evasion, with criminal fines or imprisonment for intentional tax evasion and substantially aggravated consequences for serious or organized cases. Administrative penalties, customs penalties, repayment claims, and interest may also apply where customs declarations or export evidence are inaccurate. Documentation must generally be retained for the statutory German tax-retention period, commonly eight years for accounting records and invoices under current rules, with longer periods applying to specified records.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full current-account and capital-account convertibility without exchange-control authorisations. Germany is bound by the free movement of capital under Article 63 TFEU and the OECD Code of Liberalisation of Capital Movements. The UG may freely invoice, hold, and disburse EUR and any foreign currency. Profit remittance and dividend distributions to non-resident shareholders, repayment of shareholder loans, and repatriation of capital are unrestricted from an FX perspective. The only practical hurdles are: (i) AWV statistical reporting for every cross-border payment equal to or exceeding EUR 12,500; (ii) EU/UN sanctions and embargo screening; and (iii) German dividend withholding tax (25% Kapitalertragsteuer plus 5.5% solidarity surcharge and, where applicable, church tax), which is a tax-law matter rather than an FX restriction.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Germany imposes no statutory surrender, mandatory conversion ratio, or repatriation timeline on export proceeds. Export receipts may be retained indefinitely in any currency. Nevertheless, under Section 67 AWV, each German resident must report cross-border export receipts and import payments equal to or exceeding EUR 12,500 (or the foreign-currency equivalent) to the Deutsche Bundesbank for balance-of-payments compilation, unless the correspondent bank reports on the UG's behalf under the bank reporting privilege. The UG must document in its statutory ledgers which party discharged the AWV reporting obligation for each transaction.

Central bank supervision: Deutsche Bundesbank (the central bank of the Federal Republic of Germany); the Bundesbank is the competent national authority for external trade and balance-of-payments statistics under the Foreign Trade and Payments Ordinance (Außenwirtschaftsverordnung, AWV 2013); prudential supervision of credit institutions and AML/CFT enforcement is conducted jointly with the Federal Financial Supervisory Authority (BaFin).

Stripe Fit: High

Medium. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

15% corporation tax (Körperschaftsteuer), plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on the corporation tax, producing an effective federal corporation-tax burden of 15.825%. Trade tax (Gewerbesteuer) is additional and varies by municipality; the combined corporation tax, solidarity surcharge, and trade tax burden is commonly approximately 25% to 33%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% capital-yield withholding tax plus a 5.5% solidarity surcharge on that withholding tax, producing an effective domestic withholding rate of 26.375%, before treaty or statutory exemptions.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

Generally by the end of the seventh month following the fiscal year-end. For a calendar-year taxpayer, the ordinary deadline is 31 July of the following year. Tax-advised filing generally benefits from the statutory extended deadline of the last day of February of the second following year, subject to exceptions, audits, and special administrative rules.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Generally by the end of the seventh month following the fiscal year-end; tax-advised extended deadlines may apply under the German Fiscal Code. Quarterly prepayments are generally due on 15 February, 15 May, 15 August, and 15 November.

Deadline: · Frequency:

VAT advance returns and payments are generally due by the 10th day after the reporting period. A permanent filing extension may be available on application; monthly filers generally make a special advance payment. The annual VAT return is generally due with the corporation-tax return deadline unless a different rule applies.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Generally by the 10th day after the wage-tax withholding period, with limited quarterly or annual filing eligibility based on prior-year wage tax.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Germany uses euro thresholds. A Local File is generally required for cross-border transactions with related parties when annual consideration exceeds EUR 6 million for supplies of goods or EUR 600,000 for other transactions, including services, financing, royalties, and similar transactions. A Master File is generally required for German entities belonging to a multinational group whose consolidated group revenue exceeded EUR 100 million in the preceding fiscal year. A country-by-country report is generally required for multinational groups with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million, subject to reporting-entity and exchange-of-information rules.

Economic substance scope: Germany has no single standalone economic-substance regulation equivalent to a separate ESR filing regime. Substance is assessed through the arm's-length principle, the general anti-abuse rule in Section 42 of the Fiscal Code, treaty anti-abuse provisions, Section 50d(3) of the Income Tax Act, controlled-foreign-company rules, interest-limitation rules, and beneficial-owner requirements. A foreign parent, licensor, financing company, or intermediary should have commercially credible functions, personnel, decision-making, risk control, capitalization, records, and operational capacity. A German UG must document the commercial rationale, pricing, services actually rendered, ownership of intellectual property, inventory and fulfillment functions, payment flows, and decision authority for related-party arrangements. Transfer-pricing records must generally be prepared contemporaneously and produced promptly upon request; extraordinary transactions have shorter documentation periods. Failure to provide required records may result in estimated adjustments and documentation surcharges.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: Tax evasion is generally punishable by imprisonment of up to five years or a criminal fine. Particularly serious cases may carry imprisonment from six months to ten years. Back taxes, interest, and late-payment surcharges remain payable.

Director personal liability: Managing directors may incur personal tax liability under Section 69 of the Fiscal Code for culpable failure to pay or declare taxes. Criminal exposure is personal and may include fines, imprisonment, confiscation, asset recovery, and professional consequences. Travel bans are not automatic but may arise through criminal, enforcement, or insolvency measures.
VAT Carousel, False Export, or Input-Tax Fraud

Statutory sanction: Tax evasion or aiding and abetting tax evasion may result in criminal fines or imprisonment, together with VAT reassessment, interest, refusal of input-tax deductions, and possible seizure or confiscation.

Director personal liability: Directors who knew or recklessly ignored fraud indicators may face criminal prosecution, personal tax claims, civil damages, and insolvency-related clawback exposure.
Withholding-Tax Non-Remittance

Statutory sanction: The UG may be assessed for the unremitted withholding tax, interest, and late-payment surcharges. Intentional conduct may constitute tax evasion or withholding-tax offenses and may trigger fines or imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Managing directors can be personally liable where they culpably failed to withhold or remit tax. Foreign recipients may lose treaty relief and be required to repay improperly retained benefits.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: The tax authority may adjust taxable income, deny deductions, recharacterize payments as hidden profit distributions, impose withholding tax, assess interest, and impose documentation-related surcharges. Missing or defective records may support an adverse estimate.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal civil liability for negligent tax losses and, where intentional misrepresentation exists, criminal tax liability. The corporate veil is not routinely pierced solely because a company is undercapitalized, but personal liability can arise under statutory director duties.
Hidden Profit Distribution and Improper Repatriation

Statutory sanction: The payment may be reclassified as a hidden profit distribution, denied as a deductible expense, subjected to dividend withholding tax, and included in the shareholder's taxable income. Breach of the UG statutory reserve obligation may create repayment and director-liability consequences.

Director personal liability: Directors may be required to restore unlawful payments, compensate the company, and bear tax losses. Shareholders receiving unlawful distributions may have repayment obligations.
Shell Company and Beneficial-Ownership Concealment

Statutory sanction: There is no general criminal offense merely because a company lacks substantial operations, but sham arrangements may be disregarded under the anti-abuse rule, treaty relief may be denied, taxes and interest may be assessed, and false declarations may constitute criminal offenses.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil damages, tax liability, criminal prosecution, asset seizure, and insolvency claims. False or incomplete German Transparency Register filings may result in administrative fines. Travel restrictions may be ordered only under applicable criminal or enforcement procedures, not automatically.
Anti-Money-Laundering and Payment-Compliance Failure

Statutory sanction: Depending on the regulated status and conduct, breaches may result in supervisory measures, administrative fines, reporting obligations, account restrictions, confiscation, and criminal money-laundering liability. Deliberate money laundering can carry imprisonment of up to five years or a fine, with aggravated cases carrying higher penalties.

Director personal liability: Directors and compliance officers may face personal criminal and civil exposure where they participated in, enabled, or knowingly ignored illicit transactions. Banking access and payment processing may be suspended.
Late Insolvency Filing and Wrongful Trading

Statutory sanction: Failure to file insolvency without undue delay can constitute a criminal offense, generally punishable by imprisonment of up to three years or a fine, with aggravated cases carrying higher penalties. Directors may also face repayment claims for prohibited post-insolvency payments and civil damages.

Director personal liability: Managing directors can be personally liable for prohibited payments, creditor losses, social-security arrears, and taxes. Insolvency administrators may challenge shareholder-related transactions and seek restitution.
Accounting, Recordkeeping, and ELSTER Filing Failure

Statutory sanction: The tax authority may estimate the tax base, impose late-filing surcharges, deny deductions, assess interest, and impose administrative or criminal sanctions for intentional conduct. German tax records generally require multi-year retention, with periods depending on the record type and applicable commercial and tax rules.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for avoidable tax losses and may face criminal liability for intentional false statements, obstruction, or destruction of records.
Sanctions, Export-Control, and Restricted-Party Transactions

Statutory sanction: Violations may lead to transaction prohibitions, administrative fines, criminal fines, imprisonment, asset freezing, confiscation, and loss of banking or payment access under applicable EU and German sanctions laws.

Director personal liability: Directors and responsible officers may face personal criminal and civil exposure where they authorized or negligently failed to prevent prohibited transactions. Travel restrictions may arise through criminal proceedings or enforcement orders.
